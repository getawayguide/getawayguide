The Ultimate Guide to Skopje, North Macedonia's Bizarre Capital

One of the strangest capitals in Europe, covered in faux-classical facades and the most statues in the world

Nov 9, 2026

Skopje is one of the most bizarre European capital cities, and has even earned the nickname the "Disneyland of the Balkans". That is exactly why it's worth a couple of days. The first thing you'll notice when entering the city is three massive fake pirate ships parked in the small river running through the city. As you approach the town center, statue after statue will start appearing in front of brand-new neoclassical buildings and bridges. Skopje has the most statues of any city in the world, but no one knows the real number: my walking tour guide put it at over 2,000, then admitted that the city had given up counting.

The pinnacle is a 4-story statue of Alexander the Great over a fountain (also adorned with more statues) in the city's main white marble square. The country is named after the ancient kingdom of Macedonia, but no... all of this is just over a decade old. Nearly all of it went up in the early 2010s under a massive government project called Skopje 2014. The government spent over $700 million on 136 new buildings and monuments to give the capital a classical makeover. I was hooked within an hour.

The massive towering statues of Alexander the Great in Macedonia Square, and the Stone Bridge with his father Philip II behind it

The project was intended to draw tourism (and it sure worked on me). Beyond the new facades, central Skopje is mostly communist-era concrete, rebuilt in a brutalist style after the 1963 earthquake. The city itself dates back to Roman times, and beyond Skopje 2014 there is plenty more to see: a round brutalist cathedral, the most dystopian post office on the planet and, across the river, what's often called the largest Ottoman bazaar in the Balkans outside Istanbul.

More than a quarter of the country lives here, making it the best place to get your bearings and a taste of life in North Macedonia. Most travelers I met gave the city only 24 hours (or even just half a day) before moving on to their next location. The city definitely deserves at least 2 days to fully explore and take in the strange vibes. There is nothing else like it!

A statue of Emperor Justinian I, the Stone Bridge and the National Archaeological Museum

Getting to Skopje

Bus Routes to Skopje

As the country's capital, Skopje also serves as the country's main transport hub and how you're likely getting into the country. It is home to Skopje International Airport (SKP), the country's largest airport, and the biggest bus station in this corner of the Balkans. If you're coming from a neighboring Balkan country like Albania, Serbia, or Kosovo, it's an easy overland bus ride. The websites Gjirafa Travel and FlixBus are the best places to browse bus schedules and buy tickets online. Here are the main ways to get to Skopje:

  • Flights — Skopje International Airport is 25 minutes east of the center and the main way into the country. The lovely Wizz Air flies here from across Europe, and there are many connections through the major hubs in the region like Istanbul or Vienna if you're coming from elsewhere
    • Airport bus — the easiest way to get to the town center is the WTransporter airport shuttle. It is timed to the flights and will be sitting right in front of the terminal when you arrive. The bus makes only a few stops and drops you near Macedonia Square in about 30 minutes. It costs about 200 denars ($3.50), so skip the taxi, which is a fixed 1,200 to 1,500 denars ($20 to $25)
  • Buses from Surrounding Countries — Skopje is very well connected by bus, so it's easy to string together a broader trip through the Balkans, which I highly recommend if you have more time off
    • From Albania — 5 to 6 buses a day from Tirana (5 to 6 hours with the border), from about €21 ($23) on FlixBus or Gjirafa Travel
    • From Serbia — at least 6 buses a day from Belgrade (about 6 hours), from about €26 ($29) on FlixBus
    • From Kosovo — about 9 buses a day from Pristina (about 2 hours), €8.50 to €10 ($10 to $11) on Gjirafa Travel
    • From Bulgaria — 2 to 3 buses a day from Sofia (4.5 to 5 hours), from about €27 ($30) on FlixBus or Gjirafa Travel
    • From Montenegro — 1 or 2 buses a day from Podgorica (9 to 10 hours), from about €24 ($26) on Gjirafa Travel or FlixBus
    • From Greece — only 1 or 2 direct buses a day from Thessaloniki (4 to 4.5 hours), from about €36 ($40) on FlixBus or Gjirafa Travel, so I crossed into Greece from Bitola in the south instead
  • Domestic Buses — Skopje also has buses to just about everywhere in the country. Buses to Ohrid and Bitola leave about every hour and take 3 to 4 hours, while Krushevo has only a few direct minibuses a day (3 hours). How to Get Around

Skopje is an easy city to get around, since everything is walkable. The center is small and flat with all the key attractions just a 20-minute walk from each other. For anything farther, you can also use:

  • City buses — you'll notice the red double-deckers (also part of Skopje 2014), but since everything is walkable, you'll likely never need one. If you do, rides are cheap (35 denars, well under $1) in SkopjeBus, the bus company's own app, which also plans routes and shows the buses live
  • Rideshare & Taxis — Uber and Bolt don't operate in North Macedonia. There are local apps called Wizi and BeeRide, but they aren't very good, and hailing a regular taxi is your best bet. Taxis are very cheap, but make sure to agree on a price first: even the 30-minute ride from the center out to Matka Canyon is only about 500 to 700 denars ($9 to $13) each way
The red double-deckers you'll see all over Skopje

Skopje — The City Itself

Two days gives you plenty to see and do in the "Disneyland of the Balkans." The center of Skopje is super walkable, and you won't find another city like it anywhere in the world. Explore the different sides of the city from the bronze heroes of Skopje 2014, the Ottoman Bazaar and brutalist monstrosities on the city's outskirts.

Nothing matches anything else, and that is exactly what makes walking around Skopje so much fun. Everything looks different because the city has had to start over: a magnitude 6.1 earthquake destroyed about 80% of Skopje in just 20 seconds in 1963. Yugoslavia rebuilt the city with concrete, and Skopje 2014 dressed up the center in marble half a century later. In fact, the clock on the old railway station (now the city museum) still shows that minute.

Skopje 2014 (The Funky Statues)

A government project called Skopje 2014 is why the center looks like nowhere else. The government first announced the project in 2010 to give the capital a more classical look, and within a few years it had added museums, government buildings, bridges, fountains and hundreds of bronze and marble statues. The figures scattered all across town are heroes from Macedonian history, from Alexander the Great to the revolutionaries who fought the Ottomans.

Critics called it nationalist kitsch and a waste of money in a country with high unemployment, and the next government halted it in 2018. Love it or hate it, it's what most people come to see. I personally really enjoyed it and here are the highlights:

  • Macedonia Square — located in the heart of the city, Macedonia Square is the perfect place to begin your journey. The entire square is paved in polished white marble, with a massive 4-story Alexander the Great Statue towering overhead. There's a huge fountain under it (also lined with Macedonian warriors) and a giant Skopsko beer ad behind it. Officially the statue is just called Warrior on a Horse, a dodge from the notorious naming conflict with Greece, but everyone knows it's Alexander the Great anyway
Alexander the Great towering overhead, and a bronze figure on the Bridge of Civilizations
  • The Bridge of Civilizations and the Stone Bridge — a series of bridges connects both sides of the old town over the Vardar River. The Bridge of Civilizations was one of my favorite sights in the city, adorned with dozens of statues and classical lanterns. It leads to the National Archaeological Museum, a columned building that looks like the US Capitol Building
    • Then, you have the old Ottoman Stone Bridge. It is a bit more plain, but is busy with foot traffic connecting Macedonia Square to the Old Bazaar. You'll be greeted by a giant statue of Philip II, Alexander the Great's father, and buildings with a statue in every doorway
The Bridge of Civilizations, lined with statues, leading to the columns of the Archaeological Museum
  • The Pirate Ships — the most bizarre thing in Skopje is the three fake pirate ships parked in the small river by the square. Nobody could explain why they were there (even our tour guide), and they were the first thing I saw in the city! One is now an Italian restaurant, while the other two sit abandoned, covered in graffiti and wrapped in barbed wire
One of 3 pirate ships in landlocked North Macedonia!
  • Macedonia Gate and Woman-Warrior Park — located on the south edge of Macedonia Square is a copy of the Arc de Triomphe with Macedonian murals down the sides. My guide pointed out that almost every city in the region has one and people apparently get married inside of them!?
    • Right next door sits Woman-Warrior Park, a pleasant park (also covered in statues) with a Hermes statue and a fountain. It sits right in front of a brutalist mall and an unfinished building, which sums up the city in one view
Macedonia Gate and Woman-Warrior Park, two stops on the walking tour
  • The casinos — a strange side note: I noticed that there seemed to be a casino or a sports betting shop every couple of blocks in the entire country (not just Skopje). When I asked my walking tour guide, he danced around the question, hinted at organized crime and eventually called it "just a Balkan thing". Upon more research, I discovered there are more than 1,000 casinos, slot halls and betting shops in North Macedonia (that's about 1 for every 1,800 people!)
One of more than 1,000 casinos and betting shops in North Macedonia

Guided Walking Tour

The best way to learn about North Macedonia is the free walking tour. Even by Balkan standards, North Macedonia's history and geopolitics are rather unusual, and you'd miss most of it exploring on your own. I booked Free Skopje Walking Tours, which leaves from Macedonia Square at 10 AM every day. It's tip-based (cash only) and runs 2 to 3 hours, looping past all the highlights in the city from the Woman-Warrior Park, stopping at a burek shop for a taste, and ending at the Old Bazaar.

Other sights besides statues in Skopje

Our guide was super knowledgeable, starting with the ancient Macedonians and working his way up to Yugoslavia. He covered everything from the ancient history of the country, the Yugoslavia Era, the dispute with Bulgaria over Macedonian language and identity, how a Slavic country came to claim Alexander the Great, and the Greek naming controversy. Greece put a trade embargo on the country until it changed its flag, then blocked it from NATO and the EU until it changed its name. It was a very informative tour, but not going to lie, he was a bit of an oddball with a strange sense of humor and dodged a lot of questions we had.

The Old Bazaar

The 28-meter statue of Philip II towering over the entrance to the bazaar

Cross the Stone Bridge from Macedonia Square and you're in the Old Bazaar (also known as Stara Čaršija), the city's market quarter since at least the 12th century. It is the largest bazaar outside of Istanbul and feels like you've crossed into the old Ottoman Empire with cobblestone streets, low wooden buildings, shops selling Turkish coffee and a whole street of nothing but gold jewelers, which Macedonians are apparently famous for.

A minaret over the Old Bazaar and Kebap shop

The bazaar is also the best place in town for a traditional lunch and some souvenir shopping. I really enjoyed the kebap at Kaj Гоце. Keep wandering and you'll reach Daut Pasha Hamam, a 15th-century bathhouse under 13 domes that is now the National Gallery. Finally, there is Bit Pazar, a more modern, ad hoc covered market selling clothes, shoes and fake jewelry. It's less interesting, but it's where locals actually shop.

Church of St. Constantine and Helena

The geometric stonework outside the church, and the gold iconostasis inside

The Church of St. Constantine and Helena (Hram Sv. Konstantin i Elena) was one of the most impressive churches I saw in the country. Only consecrated in September 2025, it is a refreshing, modern take with intricate geometric stonework and scaled golden domes on the outside. In general, I really enjoyed North Macedonia's churches, because they actually did something different than what you're used to seeing all over Europe.

On the inside, every surface is covered in detailed gold paintings on a blue background. The original church was built in 1927 before being demolished. The most recent iteration took 13 years and about €4 million in donations to build. The Church of St. Constantine and Helena is only one room and takes less than 15 minutes to visit. Pair it with a visit to the Mother Teresa house, located right next door.

The intricate paintings inside combined with a refreshing modern architectural style

Mother Teresa Memorial House

Mother Teresa outside her memorial house, and the glass chapel upstairs

Most people don't know that the famous Mother Teresa was born in Skopje in 1910 to a family of Kosovar-Albanian descent. The Mother Teresa Memorial House celebrates her life and famous charitable work all over the world. She's also a modern-day saint: Pope Francis canonized her as Saint Teresa of Calcutta in 2016, just 19 years after she died, and Skopje threw a week-long celebration for its hometown saint.

The museum itself is a small 2-room modern glass building with a cross and doves on top, on the site of the church where she was baptized. It's a quick 20-minute visit: one room on her life, from Skopje to Ireland to India, a chapel on the second floor and a view from the roof. Just like everything else in Skopje, the building itself is unusual, with an unorthodox layout and a mix of brutalist concrete and modernist glass.

Inside the museum

Explore Skopje's Brutalist Architecture

The Post Office tower, and the curved concrete wings beside it

Skopje is absolute heaven for fans of brutalism. If you're not familiar with it, brutalism is a style of modern architecture built from raw concrete in big, blocky geometric shapes. It was most popular from the 1950s to the 1970s, especially in the communist countries of Eastern Europe ("the Commie Block"), and it still defines much of the city today. People find it either the most interesting or the ugliest architecture they've ever seen (personally, I'm a fan).

Just minutes away from Macedonia Square sits the Central Post Office, the most brutalist building I've ever seen. A 5-story concrete tower with small ridges, shiny purple windows and air conditioners bolted to the outside. Combine that with the mob of brand-new yellow delivery vans out front, it looks like a scene out of a dystopian sci-fi movie. It's so brutalist and glorious at the same time.

A dystopian fleet of yellow delivery vans under the concrete tower

The Post Office is the highlight, but once you start looking, these concrete buildings are everywhere: the City Trade Center mall behind Woman-Warrior Park, the long apartment blocks that wall in the center, and the Transport Center, the space-age bus & train station. There are lots of other examples of brutalism around the city, but unfortunately most of them are less appealing. If brutalism is your thing, you can easily get lost on the outskirts of Skopje for a day.

The brutalist mall and statue inside Woman-Warrior Park

St. Clement of Ohrid Cathedral

The black arches and clock tower of St. Clement of Ohrid Cathedral

One of the highlights of the brutalist architecture tour is St. Clement of Ohrid. It is one of the most unusual churches I've ever been in and at the very least, I appreciate that the architects tried something different. The Archbishop Cathedral of St. Clement of Ohrid is a round church built of nothing but domes and arches. The building is symmetrical from all sides with black arches sticking out of the roof and gold mosaics set into the dark exterior. It took 18 years to build, from 1972 to 1990.

The chandelier and iconostasis inside the cathedral, and the Christ in its central dome

Inside is a single circular room, and every wall is covered in gold murals of church scenes on a deep blue background, some of the best I've seen anywhere. Look up at the Christ prominently featured in the central dome: he covers 70 square meters, and each of his eyes is 1.5 meters across. Out front is a brutalist fountain, a gift from the city's Islamic community, and across the plaza is the clock tower, a tall white column of arched windows with a clock and a gold cross on top. It's just a 10-minute walk west of Macedonia Square. Don't miss it.

Stone church with a domed roof, arches, and a fountain in the foreground, Skopje, North Macedonia

Kale Fortress

The walls and towers of Kale Fortress, and one of the communist-era stone monuments inside

On the hill above the Old Bazaar, sits Kale Fortress where Skopje began: people have lived on the hill since the 4th millennium BC. After an earthquake in 518, the emperor Justinian rebuilt the city up here, so 1963 wasn't the first time Skopje had to start over. Located only 10 minutes from the Bazaar, it is surprising that very few people visit and you'll likely have the entire place to yourself.

Walk around the castle walls and enjoy the view below. You'll notice the stark contrast between modern apartment blocks mixed with the new monuments. It is a great place to watch the sunset and worth 30 minutes (plus it's free to visit).

Skopje as a Base: The Day Trips

Skopje is a natural place to include on a Balkan trip and a great base for a few day trips. The two most popular day trips are Matka Canyon and Tetovo, both located under an hour away. It is possible to see Kosovo and Ohrid as day trips, but I highly recommend investing more time to visit both destinations. Farther south, other destinations in Macedonia including the mountain town of Krushevo and the old consular city of Bitola work better as multiday stops rather than as day trips.

Day Trips from Skopje

Matka Canyon

The massive scale of Matka Canyon

Matka Canyon is the most popular day trip from Skopje. Located about 30 minutes from the center of Skopje, the area consists of a gorge and an emerald-green lake between steep limestone walls descending from both sides. Visit St. Andrew's Monastery at the entrance, rent kayaks or take a boat ride up the lake to Vrelo Cave, one of the deepest underwater caves in Europe. The hour-long boat with the cave stop runs about 500 denars ($9), and getting into the canyon itself is free. It's easy to do on your own with a taxi or the local bus, or as a half-day tour from the city. Go early in the day in summer, since the canyon traps the heat by afternoon.

  • By bus — city bus 60 runs from the main bus station through the center and reaches the canyon in just under an hour. Check the times in the SkopjeBus app before you go and buy the 35-denar ($0.65) ticket there too
  • By taxi — about 30 minutes each way and cheap enough (roughly 500 to 700 denars, or $9 to $13) to be the simplest option, especially with a group

A fair warning that I haven't visited and Matka Canyon actually got mixed reviews from the other travelers I met. A few of them felt it wasn't very spectacular compared to what you can see in the neighboring countries, like the Albanian Alps around Theth and Valbona.

Tetovo and the Painted Mosque

The intricate painted mosque of Tetovo

The day trip I regret missing the most is to the city of Tetovo, located just 45 minutes west of Skopje by bus. The reason to go is the Painted Mosque (Šarena Džamija), a 15th-century mosque with one of the most impressive painted domes in the Balkans. The entire mosque is covered inside and out with painted flowers and geometric panels instead of the usual tiles. When it was rebuilt in 1833, more than 30,000 eggs went into the paint.

The town is also home to North Macedonia's large Albanian minority, which makes up about a quarter of the country's population. It was the main front of the 2001 insurgency, when ethnic Albanian rebels fought Macedonian security forces for autonomy and greater representation. Apparently there isn't much else to see in town, so it's a quick half day and the bus ticket only costs 120 denars ($2).

Kosovo

One of the newest countries or most contentious regions in the world (depending on who you ask) is the tiny Balkan nation of Kosovo. The capital city, Pristina, is only about 2 hours away by bus, with about 9 departures a day (check times and book on Gjirafa Travel). Being that close makes it an easy trip. There are guided day tours from Skopje to Pristina and Prizren if you'd rather not plan it.

The Bill Clinton statue in Pristina, and Prizren from its fortress at sunset

That said, Kosovo deserves at least 2 to 3 days instead of a day trip. It's a tiny country with a complicated recent history, and it takes a few days to understand the politics and see more than one city. The highlights are: Pristina for the Bill Clinton statue and National Library, and Prizren for one of the prettiest Ottoman old towns in the region, with a fortress above it with a gorgeous sunset view. The full rundown is in my Kosovo field notes.

Ohrid

Almost everyone who visits North Macedonia stops at the gorgeous Lake Ohrid, and you should too. It combines a crystal-clear lake that glows different shades of blue and green with an old town of Ottoman houses and centuries-old churches overlooking the water. Ohrid is technically doable as a day trip from Skopje, as there are guided full-day tours, but I'd recommend against it. You'd spend 7 hours on the road for a few hours in town, and you'd miss the boat tour, the best thing I did in the country. Give the lake 2 to 3 days to really take it in.

The lake is 3 to 4 hours by bus from Skopje. The ride costs 800 to 1,000 denars ($15 to $18), with buses from the main bus station about every hour (check Balkan Viator for times). Read my Lake Ohrid guide for the old town, the boat tour and where to stay.

Where to Eat

Macedonian cuisine is classic Balkan comfort food, and Skopje is a great place to get a taste. The national dish is a plate of baked beans called tavče gravče. Kebap, burek and ayran are all Macedonian staples found all across the region. For what these dishes are and what else to order, see the food section of my North Macedonia itinerary. These are the places I'd go back to:

  • Restaurant Skopski Merak — a restaurant that came highly recommended by locals for traditional Macedonian food. Located in the bohemian neighborhood of Debar Maalo, Skopski Merak is upscale and packed with locals. A plate of tavče gravče (the national dish) with bread and an ice-cold Skopsko beer was a great deal at under $10
  • Kaj Гоце — a small spot in the Old Bazaar run by a friendly older man. I wandered in at 4 PM the day I arrived, ordered the kebap and remembered why I like the Balkans so much. My favorite fast and easy meal in North Macedonia
Nothing to remind you that you're in the Balkans like a plate of Kebap
  • Burek shops — the flaky Balkan pastry filled with meat and cheese is a staple for a visit to the Balkans. Macedonians love it so much that there's a burek shop on almost every block. The one the walking tour stops at is Rekord Fresh 2, inside a concrete block building with huge Cyrillic letters outside and two guys smoking and drinking coffee out front
  • Dude Coffee — the best coffee I had in the country. Based out of a retro Victorian-style house with a nice courtyard out back. Every room has a different wacky, bohemian theme, and the whole place felt very hipster and modern. It's actually ranked No. 85 on The World's 100 Best Coffee Shops. If I'd had more time in Skopje, this is where I would've spent my downtime
The retro rooms at Dude Coffee, and a cat asleep above its outdoor courtyard
  • Cuba Coffee — another good stop for a coffee on the way through the Old Bazaar. I stopped there with a friend I'd made in Skopje on my last afternoon, and it was the right break before we wandered into the chaos of Bit Pazar

Where to Stay

Skopje is cheap enough that a whole apartment on Airbnb costs $30 to $50 a night, and every place I booked in North Macedonia fell in that range. Make sure to pick a place within walking distance of the main square and everything in this guide will be walkable from there.

I recommend staying in the Debar Maalo neighborhood. It's billed as the bohemian quarter: leafy, full of cafes and restaurants and a 10-minute walk from Macedonia Square. My studio was a well-decorated, self-contained place on a quiet street above a row of restaurants, in that $30 to $50 range, with a casino next door for good measure. You can book it on Airbnb here.
