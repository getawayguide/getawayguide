Top 10 Things to Do in North Macedonia

Experience the best that North Macedonia has to offer as one of the most overlooked countries in the Balkans

Nov 9, 2026

If I had to pick one word to describe North Macedonia, it would be bizarre, but that's exactly what makes it one of the most underrated countries in the Balkans. Most travelers skip it in favor of its more popular neighbors like Greece, Croatia or Albania, but there's something unique about North Macedonia that makes it so special. From the capital, Skopje, with the most statues in the world, to the decades-long beef with Greece over the flag and the name of the country, I found it absolutely fascinating to explore and learn more about Macedonia. The obscure history also distracts from the world-class natural characteristics like the crystal clear Lake Ohrid and the great hiking high up in the mountains.

From a traveler's perspective, North Macedonia is compact, one of the most affordable countries in Europe, and outside of Lake Ohrid in the summer you'll have sights mostly to yourself. It's worth giving the country a chance, since it's an easy add-on to a trip through more popular destinations like Greece or Albania. Here are the top ten activities I'd build a trip around, and all of them fit into my 8-day North Macedonia itinerary. Each stop has its own in-depth guide if you want more detail.

1. Take a Boat Tour of Lake Ohrid

Rowing the spring channel at St. Naum, where you can see every plant on the bottom

Undoubtedly the best activity in North Macedonia is spending a full day on the water at Lake Ohrid. Located in the southwestern corner of the country, Lake Ohrid is the most popular destination in the country. And for great reason too. The water is some of the clearest I've ever seen with different shades of green, blue and turquoise throughout. Book a full day tour to explore the Eastern shore of the lake with some of the quieter, more tranquil sides of the lake. I booked my tour with Lale Tours, a local family-run operator, with pickup, drinks and lunch included.

Trpejca from the viewpoint, a short climb above the village

The tour starts from the small village of Elshani and first passes the Bay of Bones, with a reconstruction of a 12th-century BC Bronze Age village on stilts over the water, thought to be among the earliest settlements in Europe. The tour then heads to the "Macedonian Saint Tropez" or Trpejca for the best view on the lake and on to St. Naum Monastery near the Albanian border. There, take a rowboat which glides through a spring channel fed by Lake Prespa, the spot I loved most on the lake. Lunch is a traditional Macedonian barbecue on the beach at St. Zaum. My Lake Ohrid guide covers every stop on the tour.

2. Experience the Mountain Town of Krushevo

Sunset over Krushevo, the best one I saw in the country

Macedonians kept telling me Krushevo was the one place I couldn't skip, and it became my favorite stop in the whole country. Located high in the mountains of Macedonia, Krushevo is a small mountain town of 4,000 people, with white houses and red roofs climbing both sides of a pine valley. You can walk end to end on foot in about 20 minutes so there's not a long list of sights. You come for the pace of life and get a feel for the traditional Macedonian way of life.

Townspeople casually gossip on the tiny main square, free-roaming horses graze in the park, firewood is stacked against the houses for winter and old Zastavas sit parked on the hills. You'll feel the small town vibe and understand the concept of "Balkan Hospitality" where pretty much everyone says zdravo (hello) as you pass. Watch the sunset from the terrace at Hotel Montana Palace, hike 2 hours round trip to Mechkin Kamen and give it at least one night. The buses take some planning, so read my Krushevo guide before you go.

3. Visit the Space-Age Makedonium Monument

The Makedonium in its meadow above town, and the ramp leading up to it

On the hill above Krushevo sits the Makedonium, and it feels completely out of place in a quiet mountain town. Built in 1974, it looks like a spaceship (or a giant pine cone or a COVID-19 virus, depending on who you ask) parked in a clearing in the pines, with a concrete ramp sticking out the front. It was built in honor of the Krushevo Republic which fought against about 500 years of Ottoman rule over the region.

Stained glass above the memorial, and the curving reliefs inside the Makedonium

Inside, it's completely white, with flowing reliefs on the walls, colored glass overhead and an eternal flame at its center. It perfectly encapsulates North Macedonia, an eccentric modern monument built to celebrate a storied historical legacy. The first time I went, there wasn't a soul around since it doesn't get many visitors. My Krushevo guide has the full story of the uprising.

4. Explore Skopje 2014 and the Old Bazaar

The 4-story Alexander the Great, and one of dozens of statues on the Bridge of Civilizations

Macedonia's capital, Skopje, has (rightfully) earned the title of "the Disneyland of the Balkans". Walk around the streets and it looks like someone emptied a museum of classical statues onto the streets. Between 2010 and 2018, a government project named Skopje 2014 poured more than $700 million into 136 new monuments and buildings, covering the center in bronze heroes, marble and neoclassical facades. Nobody knows exactly how many statues there are, only that Skopje has the most in the world. My walking tour guide guessed more than 2,000 and said the city itself had stopped counting.

Start on Macedonia Square under the 4-story Alexander the Great Statue (officially just "Warrior on a Horse," to avoid upsetting Greece), then cross the Stone Bridge to his father, Philip II, and loop back over the Bridge of Civilizations. Don't miss the 3 fake pirate ships in the river, one of which is now an Italian restaurant! Critics call it kitsch, and they have a point, but it's the strangest and one of the most entertaining city walks I've done in Europe.

Umbrellas strung over a lane in the Old Bazaar, and the Bit Pazar market next door

On the far side of the Stone Bridge is the Old Bazaar, the city's trading hub since at least the 12th century and often called the biggest Ottoman bazaar in the Balkans after Istanbul's. Crossing the river feels like walking into another country: historic cobblestone lanes, low wooden shops, Turkish coffee and an entire street of gold jewelers. It's also where you'll find the best cheap kebap in the city (more on that below). Visit my Skopje guide to learn more.

5. Gaze at Yugoslav and Brutalist Architecture

The Central Post Office tower, and its ribbed concrete wings

Yet another facet of Skopje is the massive concrete structures and if you're into brutalism, Skopje is a dream. The star is the Central Post Office, a 5-story tower studded with reflective purple windows and AC units, with a fleet of bright yellow delivery vans parked out front. You'll even find brutalist churches in the city with the round, black-arched Archbishop Cathedral of St. Clement of Ohrid and Church of St. Constantine and Helena with its unique geometric stone work. Both are jarring concrete structures covered on the inside in intricate, biblical frescoes of gold and deep blue. The background is a devastating earthquake flattened about 80% of Skopje in 1963, leaving the city as a blank canvas for brutalist architects to rebuild the city.

Old Yugoslav-era cars, still parked all over Krushevo

The remnants of the Yugoslav era show up outside the capital too. In Krushevo, the Makedonium is one of Yugoslavia's abstract war memorials known as spomeniks, the steep streets are still full of little Yugoslav-made cars (namely the Zastavas and the Yugo), and abandoned factories throughout the country.

6. Visit Bitola - The City of Consuls to the South

The yellow facades of Širok Sokak, Bitola's main walking street

Bitola was supposed to be a quick stop before the Greek border, and it turned into the biggest surprise of my trip. During the Ottomans, Bitola stood as one of the most important cities in Ottoman-controlled Europe, earning the nickname "the City of Consuls." It was a major city with a dozen foreign consulates, and you can still see that past on Širok Sokak, a cobblestone street of gorgeous yellow neoclassical buildings packed with cafes.

Bitola is also home to the ruins of an entire Roman town called Heraclea Lyncestis, with pristine floor mosaics, and an old bazaar where locals do their everyday shopping. Order a Turkish coffee in one of the old cafes and take your time, because nobody else is in a hurry. One full day is perfect, and it's the natural place to cross into Greece. More in my Bitola guide.

7. Wander Ohrid's Old Town to St. John Kaneo

St. John Kaneo on its cliff, and the gate on the path beside it

Ohrid's old town brings the best of Macedonia together: an Ottoman old town dating back to the 4th century BC sitting right on the shores of a gorgeous lake. It has long been an important religious center in the region and legend says it once had 365 churches, one for every day of the year. Between the Ottoman houses and marble lanes you'll find a fortress first built in the 4th century BC, a Greek theater and churches that are close to a thousand years old.

Start at Samuel's Fortress for the view over the red roofs, then head down through the forest to the famous St. John Kaneo Church, the little church on the cliff that's on every postcard of Ohrid. It's tiny, so the view is the point, surrounded on all sides by the lake. Get there early, since tour groups crowd it by late morning, then keep walking a few minutes to Labino Vista Point, where I found willows, wildflowers and a quiet getaway from the crowds. My Lake Ohrid guide maps out the whole walk.

8. Learn about Macedonia's Unique History

Macedonia Gate, Skopje's own Arc de Triomphe, and the fountain in Woman-Warrior Park

North Macedonia's history is complicated and obscure even by Balkan standards. Even if you're not a history buff, I promise you'll find it at least a little bit interesting. The country takes its name from the ancient kingdom of Alexander the Great, but its people have Slavic roots which angers Greece to no end. Greece called for an international embargo and then refused to let it join NATO or the EU until it added "North" to its name in 2019. Usually embargos are reserved for more severe crimes.... than a country's name. It made the thousands of "unnamed" statues in Skopje feel like the country was trolling Greece.

The best way to untangle all of it is the free walking tour in Skopje, which leaves Macedonia Square at 10 AM every day, runs 2 to 3 hours and works on tips. Our guide went from the ancient Macedonians through Yugoslavia to why a Slavic nation treats Alexander the Great as its own. By the end, I was happy I got a local's perspective rather than relying on the Reddit thread on North Macedonia. You'll find the tour details in my Skopje guide.

9. Explore the Ruins of Heraclea Lyncestis

The mosaic sign at the entrance, and the Roman theater built under Hadrian

Just outside Bitola lie the remains of Heraclea Lyncestis. Philip II of Macedon started the city, and the Romans turned it into a busy stop on the Via Egnatia, the road linking Europe and Asia across the Balkans. Today, you can wander its basilicas, baths, a small museum and a theater the emperor Hadrian built in the 2nd century.

Trees, birds and a grazing animal in one of the basilica floors

I'm not usually a ruins person, but the mosaics here won me over. The basilica floors are covered in near-perfect scenes of peacocks, lions, birds and trees, and the biggest stretches over 100 square meters. Tickets are 180 denars (about $3, cash only), and you can see it all in about half an hour. I shared the whole site with 2 other tourists. More on how to get there in my Bitola guide.

10. Try Macedonian Cuisine

Two Macedonian Classics: Tavče gravče and Sarma

Macedonian food is Balkan comfort food at its best: grilled meat, fresh bread, stuffed peppers and plenty of cheese, at prices were always reasonable. Start with tavče gravče, the national dish of baked beans, slow-cooked in a clay pan with onions, dried peppers and paprika until the top turns crisp and the inside is thick and smoky. Then order the kebap, small rolls of minced beef and lamb grilled over charcoal. You can find them almost anywhere in the country.

Pastries like a burek (flaky pastry with cheese or meat) are the typical breakfast in this part of the world. It is often served with ayran, the salty yogurt drink that everyone has alongside it, both going for about $2 at any bakery. Elsewhere, try polneti piperki (stuffed peppers) in Ohrid, sarma (cabbage rolls) in Krushevo, where I had some of the best food of the trip, and a slow Turkish coffee in Bitola's old bazaar. I explain every dish worth ordering in the Macedonian food guide in my itinerary.
