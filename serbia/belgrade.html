24 Hours in Belgrade: How to Spend a Day in Serbia's Capital

A day through the gold mosaics of Saint Sava, the old fortress overlooking the rivers and downtown, plus a Serbian friend's recommendations if you have more time

Nov 13, 2026

Belgrade is one of the best cities in the Balkans, and few cities in Europe have been fought over as often. Home to about 1.2 million people, it's the capital of Serbia and one of the largest cities in the region. The city sits where the Sava and the Danube rivers meet, with an old fortress on the point between them. Belgrade has a long, storied history, changing hands between many empires over the centuries, including the Romans, the Byzantines and the Ottomans. It has been fought over in 115 wars and razed 44 times. For more than a century, the Sava split the city between two empires: the Austrians ruled the west bank, including the town of Zemun, and the Ottomans held the fortress and the old city on the east bank.

Today, the city is clean, modern and full of warm, down-to-earth people, and the hospitality ended up being the highlight of my trip. My taxi driver from the airport, a really nice older man, talked the whole way into the city, explaining that Serbian is written in two alphabets, Cyrillic and Latin, and wouldn't let me out of the taxi until I'd written down his tips for Belgrade. Be sure to visit the iconic Church of Saint Sava, a towering white marble church with green domes and gold mosaics covering the inside. It's one of the most impressive churches I've ever been in.

The Church of Saint Sava peeking through the trees and Republic Square

I wrote this guide for people who only have 24 hours in the city, whether you have an extended layover or are just passing by. However, give Belgrade 3 to 5 days, and go on a weekend if you can. What I saw of Belgrade was just the tip of the iceberg. I never made it to the Museum of Yugoslavia, where Tito is buried, or on any of the day trips around Serbia, and missed some of the best nightlife in Europe. Spend the first day on the walk I did through the old center, then add the places in More to Do in Belgrade, like Zemun, an old Habsburg town across the river, or a day trip to Novi Sad. There's plenty more to see in Serbia, and I'm definitely planning to go back for another trip.

Unfortunately, I had really limited time in Serbia, so instead this guide is filled with recommendations from a Serbian friend who lives in Belgrade. Most of the food, bars, hotels and day trips are a local's picks, not a tourist list, and I say so wherever it applies.

Getting to Belgrade

Bus Routes to Belgrade

Belgrade is the easiest city in Serbia to get to, with flights from all over Europe and direct buses from every major city around it. Most people fly into Belgrade Nikola Tesla Airport, the country's main airport. With over 100 destinations (including nonstops to New York & Chicago), Air Serbia singlehandedly makes Belgrade a popular hub in the area.

Long-distance buses arrive at the new main bus station, about 15 minutes from the old center by taxi app. You can book tickets online through BusTicket4.me, which covers most of the regional routes, or FlixBus, which operates the longer international routes. Here are the main ways to get to Belgrade:

  • From the airport — the airport is about 18 km west of downtown. The A1 minibus runs to Slavija Square in about 30 minutes for 400 dinars (about $4), paid in cash, and city bus 72 to Zeleni Venac is free
  • By Train — the high-speed train from Novi Sad is the fastest way in. Trains leave the Novi Sad train station at least once an hour for Beograd Centar and take about 40 minutes, for about 680 dinars (about $7). Buses take 1 to 1.5 hours for a similar price
  • By Bus
    • From Sarajevo, Bosnia — combine your trip with the rest of Bosnia! Buses leave the East Sarajevo bus station several times a day and take 7 to 9 hours, for 3,000 to 4,500 dinars ($30 to $45)
    • From Skopje, North Macedonia — about five buses a day leave the Skopje bus station and take 6 to 7.5 hours, for 2,500 to 4,500 dinars ($25 to $45)
    • From Zagreb, Croatia — FlixBus leaves the Zagreb bus station about every 4 hours and takes about 5.5 hours, for 3,500 to 6,000 dinars ($35 to $60)
    • From Budapest, Hungary — buses leave Népliget bus station daily and take about 6 hours, for about 4,000 dinars ($40). The direct train, suspended since 2019 for the rebuild, is expected back as a 3-hour ride in December 2026
    • From Podgorica, Montenegro — buses leave the Podgorica bus station several times a day and take 7 to 10 hours, for 2,500 to 5,000 dinars ($25 to $50). The overnight train from Bar is the scenic option
    • From Sofia, Bulgaria — a bus or two a day leave the Serdika bus station and take 5 to 6 hours, for 2,500 to 5,000 dinars ($25 to $50)
    • From Prishtina, Kosovo — two to four buses a day leave the Prishtina bus station and take about 6 hours, for about 3,000 dinars ($30). Only take it if you entered Kosovo from Serbia (see Crossing from Kosovo)

Getting around Belgrade

Everything in the old center is walkable. For anything farther, you can also use:

  • Public transport — Belgrade has very clean and organized public transport, a network of buses, trams and trolleybuses that has been free for everyone since January 2025. Routes and timetables are on the city's transit site, BG Prevoz
  • Ridesharing apps — on a short visit, the ridesharing apps save a lot of time. Uber and Bolt don't operate in Serbia, so download Car:Go or Yandex Go before you land. At the airport, the kiosks in the baggage hall also print a fixed-price taxi voucher for your zone of the city
  • Language — the level of English is very high with almost everyone I met speaking fluent English. Signs everywhere are in both Cyrillic and Latin, so you can read the street names and I had no trouble getting around
  • Money — the official currency is the Serbian dinar (not the euro) at about 100 dinars to the US dollar. Cards are widely accepted in Belgrade, but carry some cash for bakeries, small cafés and the airport minibus
  • Safety — for a lot of people, Serbia still brings up images of the Balkan wars of the 1990s, and it's often unclear where the country stands between Russia and the West. Belgrade felt very safe when I visited. I walked around alone from before 7 AM, and even with nationwide protests going on, I never felt uneasy (more on that later)

Crossing from Kosovo

Don't bring up Kosovo, since it is still a very contentious issue. Long story short, Kosovo declared independence from Serbia in 2008, but Serbia still considers it part of its territory. Only about 57% of the world's countries recognize Kosovo, 110 of the 193 UN members. That makes the border one-way for most travelers. You can go from Serbia into Kosovo and back, but if you start in Kosovo, say from Albania or North Macedonia, Serbia will likely turn you away at the crossing. A bit confusing, I know... the best way to avoid this border is to just go around through North Macedonia, Montenegro or Albania. More in my Kosovo field notes.

The Student Protests

I visited Serbia at a very unique time in July 2025 when Serbia was in the middle of the biggest protest movement in its history. I almost skipped Serbia entirely because of the protests, since I thought it would be unsafe due to the scale of the protest (over 300,000 people filling the streets of Belgrade). I'm glad I didn't. Just like in Georgia, the protests had no effect on how safe it felt, and the politics never got in the way of my day. With the recent 2026 political elections and change of power, this chapter of the country's history is already over and will not affect your trip. However, it is pivotal to understanding modern day Serbia and the issues it deals with.

On November 1, 2024, a newly renovated concrete roof at the Novi Sad railway station, in Serbia's second largest city, collapsed, killing 16 people. Students blamed government corruption in the renovation, blockaded their universities and took to the streets, and the protests spread to towns all over the country. The biggest one, in Belgrade on March 15, 2025, drew an estimated 275,000 to 325,000 people, the largest rally Serbia has ever seen. The protests were overwhelmingly peaceful, though there were clashes with police and government supporters in the summer of 2025. They continued for about two years, until the Student List, a slate of candidates put forward by the student movement itself, won an absolute majority in the parliamentary election in October 2026.

A shop window reading “Students in Blockade,” and the tents in Pionirski Park

When I was there, the signs of the protests were everywhere. There was graffiti all over Republic Square, and some of it was nationalist and inflammatory. Across the street from Parliament, Pionirski Park was full of tents with words spray-painted on them in Serbian. I assumed they belonged to the protesters, but that camp was actually set up in March 2025 by supporters of the ruling party who opposed the protesters. On election night in October 2026, celebrating crowds took the pro-government camp apart. Enough politics! Let's continue to plan your upcoming Belgrade trip.

Belgrade — The City Itself

If you only have 24 hours in Belgrade, there is enough time to see all the highlights, and luckily most of them are within walking distance of each other. You'll spend most of your time in the old town which meets at the intersection of two of Europe's largest rivers. Make a loop through the center, starting at Republic Square, walking down the beautiful walking street of Kneza Mihaila and over to the older side of Belgrade called Kosančićev Venac. Then double back past Hotel Moskva and Parliament to the Church of Saint Sava. From there, take a ride to the Belgrade Fortress to catch the sunset over the river, and finish with dinner in Skadarlija. Here's how I'd spend the day:

Republic Square

A statue of Prince Michael in front of the National Museum in Republic Square

The best place to start your time in Belgrade is right in the center at the historic Republic Square. The National Museum of Serbia sits on one side, the National Theatre on another, and the statue in the middle is Prince Michael, who drove the last Ottoman garrisons out of Serbia's cities in 1867. The names of the cities he won back are carved on the pedestal, and his outstretched arm points toward the Serbian lands still under Ottoman rule at the time. Locals just call it kod konja, “at the horse,” and it's one of the city's classic meeting spots.

The square itself is where the Stambol Gate stood, the Ottoman gate on the road to Istanbul, where the Ottomans executed their Christian subjects by impaling them, until Prince Michael had it torn down in 1866. The square also opens onto the start of Kneza Mihaila, the city's main walking street and our next stop on the list.

Kneza Mihaila

From the square, I walked down Kneza Mihaila, the main pedestrian street of the old center. The story has it that Kneza Mihaila still follows the main street of Singidunum, the Roman town that once stood here. Today, the city center is very modern and polished. The street runs for about a kilometer down to the fortress, lined with grand buildings from the late 1800s that are now shops, restaurants and cafés with tables out front.

Kneza Mihaila before 7 AM, a short walk from Republic Square

Locals gather here to do shopping, grab dinner and meet for coffee. It was protected as a historic area in 1964, the first in Belgrade. This is the stretch where Belgrade started to seem like one of the nicest capitals in the Balkans to me, a sharp contrast to the grittier Balkan capitals. I stopped here early in the morning and there was barely anyone around. I stopped for coffee along the way with some Turkish bread with sesame seeds and olives inside.

A few minutes' walk from Republic Square, I also made it to Skadarlija, the other famous walking street in the center and the more historic one. Where Kneza Mihaila is modern and polished, Skadarlija is the city's old bohemian quarter, a short cobblestone street of old taverns, restaurants and shops. It's a natural place for dinner on your first night, and Tri šešira, a kafana (a traditional Serbian tavern) open since 1864, is the classic restaurant in the area.

Kosančićev Venac

The historic side of Belgrade: a corner café on Kosančićev Venac and an old Triumph

Just a few minutes west on foot is Kosančićev Venac, the oldest neighborhood in Belgrade. It's a short detour off Kneza Mihaila, and worth it for the lookout where it meets Kralja Petra, said to be one of the most beautiful in Belgrade. Roam around to get a sense of historic Belgrade. I stumbled upon a classic Triumph car and Skica, a tiny corner café, with “Škola života” (school of life) on the sign and its green wooden doors swung open onto old stools and a wooden staircase.

The bell tower of the Cathedral of St. Michael the Archangel and a park nearby

Along the way you pass the Residence of Princess Ljubica, an 1830s mansion that is now a museum, and the ruins of the National Library, bombed by the Germans in April 1941 and left as a memorial. Just up the street is the Cathedral Church of St. Michael the Archangel, whose tall bell tower you'll see from Kosančićev Venac.

The Church of Saint Sava

The Church of Saint Sava through the trees

The next stop is the symbol of Belgrade, the iconic Serbian Orthodox cathedral named Church of Saint Sava. Located in a lush green park in the middle of the city, the church towers over you at 257 feet (78 meters) tall. It is the fourth largest Eastern Orthodox church in the world. Construction started in 1935, and the interior mosaics were only finished in 2020. It stands where the Ottomans burned the relics of Saint Sava, the founder of the Serbian church, in 1595. Saint Sava became a symbol of resistance for Orthodox Serbs, who lived under Ottoman rule for about four centuries.

The inside of Saint Sava, covered in gold mosaics and green marble inside

The outside is impressive, but the inside is home to the reason to come. The whole interior is covered floor to ceiling in intricate gold mosaics, and the dome alone supposedly cost $10 million to construct. In all, the mosaics cover about 15,000 square meters, roughly two soccer fields. It is free to visit and takes a half hour to fully explore. I'd put the church at the top of any list for Belgrade, even if you only have an hour.

Hotel Moskva

Hotel Moskva on Terazije, home of the Moskva šnit

Another iconic stop is the Hotel Moskva, a historic, grand old hotel that has been open since 1908. The hotel is unmissable on Terazije, the square at the very center of Belgrade, with its unique architecture and two spires sticking out of the roof. Hotel Moskva is famous for the Moskva šnit, a layered almond sponge cake with cream and cherries that the hotel's pastry shop introduced in 1974. Its guests over the years have included Albert Einstein, Jean-Paul Sartre and Leon Trotsky. I walked past it at 7AM and it felt too early in the morning for cake, so I unfortunately skipped it. If you come later in the day, be sure to sit down for a slice!

The House of the National Assembly

The national assembly building in Belgrade

The Serbian Parliament, or the House of the National Assembly, is a short 10-minute walk from old town. It is a grand stone building with a row of green domes on top, and the Serbian flags lined up out front make it hard to miss. It took almost 30 years to finish: Konstantin Jovanović drew the first plans in 1891, work started in 1907, and it opened in 1936. You can see inside both the Parliament and the New Palace on a guided tour on the first Saturday of each month, but you'll need to sign up ahead of time with your passport at the tourist information center on Kneza Mihaila.

Sitting right across the street from Parliament is Pionirski Park. The park started out as the royal garden of the two palaces beside it, the Old Palace (now Belgrade's City Hall) and the New Palace (now the office of the President of Serbia). When I was there, it was full of tents put up by ruling-party supporters during the student protests, and the police kept it fenced off from March 2025 until election day in October 2026.

Belgrade Fortress & Kalemegdan

The Inner Stambol Gate, the main gate into the fortress, and the twin towers of Despot's Gate

Located on a hill overlooking the meeting of the Sava and Danube rivers is the almost 2,000-year-old Belgrade Fortress. It is located inside a massive park called Kalemegdan which holds the best view in Belgrade, the fortress itself, the Military Museum and the Belgrade Zoo (open since 1936), plus a small amusement park and Dino Park, a dinosaur park for kids. The fortress' history is inextricably linked to the city's history dating all the way back to the 3rd century BC when the Celts settled the area. The Romans built the first stone fort, and over the next 2,000 years the Byzantines, Hungarians, Serbs, Ottomans and Austrians all took their turn holding it.

The clock tower above the Clock Gate, and a row of missile launchers below the walls

Belgrade Fortress stands as a huge white brick fortress with a big courtyard inside. There are several different entrances including the popular Despot's Gate, an entrance with two massive towers and a tiny doorway which dates back to the early 1400s and is the best-preserved medieval part of the fortress. The whole fortress has not been fully restored, but what caught my eye was the moat, which is lined with old tanks, missile launchers and cannons. From the walls, you look down on the spot where the Sava meets the Danube, which is the view to come for.

A view of the intersection of Sava and Danube from Belgrade Fortress

More to Do in Belgrade

There's plenty more to do in Belgrade, easily enough to justify 2 to 5 days, or even a week. These are the places I'm planning to see on my next trip, and they're the ones my friend from Belgrade pointed me to:

  • Museum of Yugoslavia — Belgrade was the capital of Yugoslavia, and this is where Josip Broz Tito, the country's national hero and longtime leader, is buried. The history of Yugoslavia is very recent and very interesting, and the museum is definitely worth a visit, at about 800 dinars (about $8).
  • Nikola Tesla Museum — dedicated to the famous inventor, who was ethnically Serbian. He's beloved in Serbia as one of the country's most famous exports and the airport is named after him too. Inside are his personal belongings (even his suit), copies of his inventions like the induction motor and the Tesla coil, which the guides switch on during the tour, and the urn that holds his ashes. It's a short walk from the Church of Saint Sava, so it fits on the same morning
  • Zemun — across the river from central Belgrade is a neighborhood called Zemun. The highlight is the Gardoš Tower, which the Hungarians built in 1896 to celebrate a thousand years in the region, back when Zemun was on the Habsburg side of the border. You can climb it for a couple of dollars for a view over Zemun and the Danube. Two of the food picks below are restaurants by the river in Zemun, Restoran Šaran and Zora, so it makes a good afternoon and dinner.
  • 25. Maj — the riverfront promenade along the Danube where locals go for a walk. In summer, the sports center on the promenade opens its outdoor pools, with lounge chairs right by the river
  • Ada Ciganlija — there is actually a strong beach culture in Belgrade! Ada Ciganlija is where the city heads in the summer to sunbathe and swim off its beaches. Locals jokingly call it the Belgrade Sea, and if you're here in the summer, it's a great place to relax and spend an afternoon

Day Trips from Belgrade

Most of Serbia's highlights are within just a few hours of Belgrade and the country has an extensive public transport system making day trips a breeze. Every local I met during my trip advised me for my next trip to head for the mountains in the south and to visit the historic cities in the north. Avala, a forested mountain just south of the city, is the closest, and the places farther out are better to stay for a night or two:

Day Trips Around Serbia
  • Avala — a 511-meter (1,677-foot) mountain about 30 minutes south of Belgrade, known for its TV tower, which has a view over the entire city. The tower was destroyed in the 1999 NATO bombing and finally rebuilt in 2010. At 205 meters (672 feet), it's the tallest tower in Serbia, and an elevator takes you up to the observation deck at 122 meters for about 300 dinars ($3)
    • Almost 70% of the mountain is forest, with hiking trails up to the Monument to the Unknown Hero at the summit. It's been Belgrade's classic picnic spot for generations, and it's the easiest escape from the city if you want a few hours in the woods
  • Novi Sad — the second largest city in Serbia is only about 40 minutes from Belgrade on the high-speed train, so it's the easiest trip out. Walk up to the star-shaped Petrovaradin Fortress above the Danube for the view back over the city.
    • Look for the clock tower, where the hands are reversed, with the big hand showing the hours, so that fishermen on the river could read it from far away. The fortress is also home to EXIT, one of the biggest music festivals in Europe, and its old walls sound like a pretty epic place for a festival. Back across the river, the old town is worth a walk, and Dunavska Street is known for its ice cream shops
The Church of the Name of Mary and the surrounding buildings in Novi Sad
  • Sombor — a beautiful, historic city in the far north, close to the Hungarian and Croatian borders. Nikola Jokić is from here and you'll see lots of murals celebrating him throughout the city! It's about 2.5 hours northwest of Belgrade by car
  • Krupajsko vrelo — a karst spring in central Serbia, where the Krupaja River comes up out of a cave into a small lake of very clear water, then spills over a low dam into a string of little waterfalls. The water stays a chilly 9 to 11°C (about 50°F). It's about half an hour's drive from Manasija, a walled 15th-century monastery, so the two make one long day by car
  • Manasija Monastery — considered one of the prettiest monasteries in the country, located in central Serbia. Despot Stefan Lazarević built it in the early 1400s inside walls with 11 towers, so it looks more like a castle than a monastery. Inside the walls, the church still has part of its original 15th-century frescoes, and the monks here were known across the Balkans for their manuscripts and translations. It's about 2 hours south of Belgrade
The stone towers and church at Manasija Monastery in central Serbia
  • Kopaonik — known as the best place to ski in Serbia, and doubles as a great hiking spot in the summertime. Located in the far south of the country, along the border with Kosovo (about 4 hours from Belgrade by car). Its highest peak, Pančić's Peak, reaches 2,017 meters, and the mountain is a national park
  • Kosovo — one of the most interesting and contentious countries in the Balkans. Buses from Belgrade reach Prishtina in about 6 hours, and since you'd be entering from Serbia, you can come back the same way (see Crossing from Kosovo). It deserves 2 to 3 days instead of a day trip, to understand the politics and to see the beautiful Ottoman old town of Prizren
The highlights of Kosovo: Bill Clinton statue in Prishtina, and Prizren from its fortress at sunset

Where to Eat

Serbian food is similar to many Balkan cuisines, filled with hearty dishes characterized by spiced grilled meat, flaky pastries and stuffed cabbage. Belgrade is more expensive than the rest of the country, with a meal at a casual restaurant running about 1,400 dinars (about $14). Start with the Serbian classics, which you'll find all over the city:

  • Burek — a flaky Balkan pastry filled with meat, cheese, potatoes or spinach. You can find it at almost any bakery
  • Ćevapi — little spiced grilled sausages of minced meat, found all over the Balkans and typically served with fries
  • Sarma — cabbage leaves stuffed with rice and minced meat

My friend from Belgrade recommended most of the following places to try while you're there:

  • Restoran Vuk — a traditional Serbian restaurant in the city center, close to Kneza Mihaila. It serves Balkan home cooking and grilled meat in an old-Belgrade dining room full of locals, at about 2,000 dinars ($20) a person, and it's worth reserving for dinner
  • Restoran Šaran — a fancy Serbian restaurant right on the waterfront in Zemun. It's an old fish tavern on the Danube quay below Gardoš. The restaurant's specialties are its fisherman's soup (riblja čorba) and pan-fried Danube carp, which is what šaran means. Expect about 4,500 dinars ($45) a person
  • Zora Sweets & Desserts — a pastry shop on Glavna, the main street of Zemun, a few minutes' walk from the river and the climb to Gardoš Tower. Open for more than 50 years, it's the oldest pastry shop in Zemun, and the old-school favorites are the chestnut purée, žito (sweet boiled wheat with whipped cream) and krempita (a custard cream slice)
  • Hotel Moskva — the historic four-star hotel on Terazije, where the café has served the Moskva šnit since 1974, a layered almond cake with cream and cherries
  • Pizza Bar Vračar — an upscale cafe located right across the street from the Church of Saint Sava. There is a ton of outdoor seating here, making it a great place to relax in-between exploring sights

Nightlife in Belgrade

Belgrade is considered one of the best party cities in Europe, and the nightlife is supposedly world-class. The city has everything from floating clubs, famous guest DJ's and cocktail bars. In summer, a lot of it moves onto the splavovi, the floating clubs lined up along the Sava and the Danube. The most popular ones are Freestyler, Lasta and 20/44. It's not a secret that Serbians know how to party, so plan your trip around a weekend if you can. I didn't get to see any of it on my short visit, but these are the two cocktail bars my friend pointed me to:

  • Druid Bar — a tiny speakeasy behind an unmarked door near the fortress end of Kneza Mihaila. There's no menu, so tell the bartender what you like, and leave your phone in your pocket: it's a no-Wi-Fi, no-selfie bar
  • Riddle Bar — a wood-paneled cocktail bar on the main street of Skadarlija and a spot on World's 50 Best Discovery. Cocktails run about 1,000 to 1,600 dinars ($10 to $16), and book ahead on a Friday or Saturday
The keyhole mural behind the bar at Riddle, a woman in a blue hat with a finger to her lips

Where to Stay

Stay in the old center, between Republic Square and Belgrade Fortress, so most of this guide is within walking distance. My time in Belgrade was really limited, so I can't vouch for a hotel myself. These are the two my friend recommended, and both are in the center:

  • Balkan Soul Hostel — considered the best hostel in Belgrade, a few minutes from Kosančićev Venac, with dorm beds from about 2,000 dinars ($20) a night
  • Yugodom — a retro guesthouse on Strahinjića Bana that markets itself as the world's first and only “stay-over museum,” near Skadarlija. It opened in 2013 and each room is dedicated to a different decade of Yugoslavia and named after a cult Yugoslav movie. Everything inside, from the furniture to the records, magazines and kitchenware, was made in Yugoslavia. It's more of an interesting place to stay than a luxury experience, but it's the closest you'll get to a hotel room from the Yugoslav times!
