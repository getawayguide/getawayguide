New Zealand Travel Guide with Perfect 21 Day Itinerary

Everything you need to plan the ultimate road trip through New Zealand, from geothermal Rotorua and Hobbiton to the glaciers and fjords of the South Island

Coming 2026

New Zealand is, without question, one of the most breathtaking countries on earth and personally my favorite country in the world. Located in the remote South Pacific, the country is made up of two main islands: the North Island (Te Ika-a-Māui) and the South Island (Te Waipounamu). Packed into these two small islands is a wide range of the most magnificent landscapes in the world including active volcanoes, pristine fjords, alpine glaciers, golden beaches, rolling green farmland and geothermal hot springs. I can't even begin to explain how incredible the landscapes are there. 

The magic of New Zealand comes from its remote location deep in the Southern Hemisphere. Even its closest neighbor, Australia, is a 3-hour flight away. Both the wildlife and nature of New Zealand developed in isolation from the rest of the world with the first humans arriving in New Zealand as late as the 13th century. This is what makes the landscapes and wildlife so unique (especially the cute little kiwi bird!). At times, it even felt like I was on another planet.

Whether you're an avid hiker, an adrenaline junkie, or just looking to soak in some of the most jaw-dropping scenery on earth, New Zealand has something for everyone. A few things worth knowing before you go:

  • Nature > Cities - The magic of New Zealand is in its breathtaking landscapes. The cities and towns are pleasant, but not particularly impressive. Plan your trip around the outdoor activities and hikes
  • Culture - Besides small differences like driving on the left side of the road and sayings like sweet as, New Zealand is a very comfortable, familiar place to travel. You’ll find that Kiwis are some of the most friendly and laid-back people in the world. The country has also done a wonderful job at embracing local Māori culture if you’re looking for a more unique cultural experience
  • Cost - New Zealand is an expensive place to travel, comparable to traveling in the U.S., Canada or Western Europe. However, there are ways to save money including buying groceries, camping or renting a campervan
  • Type of Experience - There are many ways to experience New Zealand ranging from staying in fancy ski resorts, backpacking for weeks on end or even living out of a campervan. If you're looking for a more social experience, I'd recommend the Kiwi Bus — a hop-on hop-off bus that connects all the major stops. I've written about my experience in detail here.

This travel guide covers everything you need to know before visiting New Zealand and lays out a suggested 21-day itinerary covering both islands with honest takes on what's worth your time and what you can skip.

What to know before visiting New Zealand

☀️Best Time to Go

Being in the Southern Hemisphere, the seasons are flipped. Best time to visit is the summer (December to February) for warm weather and long days or conversely during the Ski Season (June to October)

📅How Long?

3-4 weeks to cover both islands at a comfortable pace; you could spend up to 6 months exploring the country

💵Daily Budget

New Zealand is an expensive country to travel. You can get by on USD $50–80/day as a budget traveler; mid-range travelers should expect NZ$150–250/day

🛂Visa Requirements

The NZeTA (a simplified visa) is required for many nationalities including the U.S., U.K., EU and Australia. Citizens of other countries may need a full visa

✈️Getting There

Fly into Auckland (AKL) for the North Island and Queenstown (ZQN) or Christchurch (CHC) for the South Island

🚗Getting Around

Rent a car or campervan if you want to explore at your own pace. InterCity buses connect major towns, domestic flights save time between islands, and the Kiwi Bus if you're looking for a social backpacking experience

💰Currency

New Zealand Dollar (NZD) is the official currency. Cards are accepted everywhere so you won't need cash

🌐Language

English and Māori are the official languages. You'll encounter Māori in place names, greetings and signs throughout the country

Why Visit New Zealand?

[picture pair]

There are many reasons why New Zealand is my favorite country and why I commonly recommend it to my family and friends looking for their next adventure. It was actually the first country I recommended to my parents after coming home from a year of travel! If I were to narrow down the three main reasons that set New Zealand apart, they would be:

  • Beauty and Diversity of the Landscape— If I could only give one answer, this would be it! I was absolutely blown away by the nature in New Zealand, unlike anywhere else I've ever been. The best example of this is the Tongariro Crossing hike. In just eight hours: I started by hiking in a barren lava field covered in black volcanic ash, then climbed along a misty rim of an active volcano, admired blue-green volcanic pools, walked on hard orange clay (which felt like I was walking on Mars) and then appeared in a lush green rainforest. There's a reason why fantasy movies like Lord of the Rings and The Chronicles of Narnia were all filmed here 
  • Comfort and Familiarity — New Zealand is a very comfortable place to travel with a lot of the creature comforts of home and is consistently ranked as one of the top three safest countries in the world. As a Westerner or English speaker, there is no sense of culture shock. The familiarity and comfort of New Zealand makes it the type of destination where you can spend months and also suitable for all types of travelers including families or less adventurous travelers. Beyond that, Kiwis are extremely friendly and will go out of their way to strike up a conversation or help you if you're lost
  • Adventure Capital — There is no shortage of adventure sports in New Zealand. Whether it is bungee jumping, skydiving, white-water rafting, heli-skiing or jet boating, you'll find it offered somewhere on these two islands. Queenstown actually invented modern adventure tourism and the country has been pushing the boundaries of adventure ever since

Top Things to Do

The hardest part about visiting New Zealand is deciding what to do. After three weeks in the country, I felt like I barely scratched the surface. Here are the top five experiences from my time in the country:

  • Tongariro Alpine Crossing — probably the best hike I've ever done. This 19 km (or 12 mile) one-way crossing takes 7–9 hours to complete. It traverses the rim of an active volcano through six distinct ecosystems in a single day. Emerald crater lakes, steaming vents, black volcanic lava fields and sweeping mountain views that are genuinely unlike anything else. You can either drive here or arrange a shuttle from the nearby town of Taupo
  • Milford Sound — located in the far southern part of New Zealand, Milford Sound is often called the eighth natural wonder of the world. It is defined by a narrow fjord flanked by sheer cliff faces with massive ten-story waterfalls cascading from all sides. The best way to experience it is a scenic boat ride through the sound. The area gets rain about 300 days a year so it is almost impossible to plan for a sunny day. Wet days actually make the waterfalls stronger, so don't let the weather put you off
  • Franz Josef Heli-Hike — this is a truly once-in-a-lifetime experience: getting suited up in climbing gear, taking a specialized helicopter through the mountains and up to the glacier and then following our guide across the massive 12-kilometer glacier. The breathtaking view of the Southern Alps from the helicopter combined with the deep blue glow of the ice formations was absolutely incredible. For safety reasons, the activity is highly weather-dependent and can be canceled with little notice so keep your schedule flexible
  • White Water Rafting in Rotorua — it should be no surprise by now that the highest-grade (aka most dangerous) white water rafting in the world is located in New Zealand. If you're looking for an adrenaline fix, this is the place to go, especially when you hit the 8-meter waterfall drop on one of the runs
  • Hobbiton Movie Set — located in a small town on the North Island between Auckland and Rotorua, Hobbiton is a magnet for all fans of the Lord of the Rings series worldwide. Even if you haven't seen the movies, it was a fascinating look behind the scenes of moviemaking and the sheer dedication to detail was impressive. Look for the two million dollar tree that was recreated for the Hobbit movie!
This is just the beginning! Read my article about the Top 10 things to do in New Zealand for more.

The Route: Suggested 21 Day Itinerary

Three weeks is the ideal amount of time to explore both islands without feeling rushed. The itinerary below starts in Auckland, works its way through the North Island from Coromandel, Waitomo, Rotorua and Taupo, crosses to the South Island by ferry, and ends in Queenstown (the most common exit point for international flights from the South Island). This route takes into account transit times and only includes this highlights from my time in New Zealand.

[map embed]
1–2
Auckland
Start your trip by flying into Auckland International Airport (AKL). As New Zealand's largest city, Auckland's highlights can comfortably be covered in two days. Begin by exploring the waterfront harbor and the hip neighborhood of Ponsonby, known for its cafes, boutiques and buzzing restaurant scene. In the evening, head to Mount Eden. Located in the heart of Auckland, this dormant volcano is now covered in a thick layer of green grass and has some of the best sunset views from its crater rim. 

On your second day, take the short ferry from the harbor to Waiheke Island. Hop on the hop-on hop-off bus or rent a bike to explore the island's many vineyards, beautiful beaches and charming downtown at your own pace.

3
Coromandel & Hot Water Beach
Next, head east from Auckland to the Coromandel Peninsula, a popular holiday getaway for Kiwi's. Famous for it's beautiful clear blue water contrasted with towering white cliffs. The best way to experience this is a guided kayak tour of Cathedral Cove. The water is relatively calm and you'll have the freedom to explore the sea caves and arches across the coastline. There area is a protected fish breeding area so you'll encounter an abundance of marine life like large snappers and dolphins.  In the evening, head to Hot Water Beach where you can dig your own hot tub at this beach! 4
Waitomo Caves
The next stop is the Ruakuri Caves in the small town of Waitomo. These caves are known for the glowworms that cover the ceilings. There are millions of glowworms that produce a brilliant blue-white bioluminescent glow, making the dark caves completely light up with small lights that look like little florescent LEDs. You can either take a standard 1 hour walking tour or go on the black water rafting tour. This is a canyoning experience that takes you through the cave system on an inflatable tube, floating through complete darkness under a canopy of glowworms. Look up pictures before you go to see if you're interested!

5–8
Rotorua — Geothermal Wonders and Māori Culture
Rotorua is the most activity-packed spot on the North Island and I'd recommend spending at least 4 days here. The town sits on a massive volcanic hot spot and is also the cultural heart of Māori New Zealand. Here are some of the key highlights:

Hobbiton — For some, this may be the main reason you're visiting the country. Originally a sheep farm outside the small town of Matamata, the Shire set from The Lord of the Rings and The Hobbit is home to 44 unique Hobbit holes (cute little Hobbit homes). The surrounding area is very serene and peaceful adding to the fantastical feel of Hobbiton. Even if you haven't seen the movies, I still highly recommend visiting to get a peak behind one of the most expensive movie productions in history, costing over $[ ] to build. You can even go inside some of the Hobbit holes and be sure to make a stop at the Green Dragon Inn at the end to have some [Hobbit beer]!

White water rafting — Rotorua has the highest-grade white water in the world that you can do as an amateur. The highlight is an 8-meter waterfall drop on the main run. I went with [Name of company] and they were highly professional. Highly recommended, especially if you only do one adventure activity on the North Island.

Kuirau Park — this is a set of geothermal hot springs located right in the middle of town, with bubbling mud pools, steam vents and pools of boiling water. The view of the steam rising from the geysers was absolutely incredible at sunset. I preferred it over Te Puia, the more famous geyser in Rotorua.

There is also much more to see including the Rotorua Government Gardens and the Kiwi Hatchery. I've written in much more detail in my [Ulimate Rotorua Guide].

9–11
Taupo & Tongariro Alpine Crossing
Taupo is a small, pleasant lakeside town on the shores of Lake Taupo, New Zealand's largest lake. The town itself is really small, but it's an excellent place to spend two days:

Tongariro Alpine Crossing is probably the best hike I've ever done. This 19 km one-way trail takes 7–9 hours and crosses the rim of an active volcano through six completely different ecosystems in a single day, from dense bush to barren lava fields to volcanic tundra. The emerald-coloured crater lakes, the steaming vents and the scale of the volcanic landscape are breathtaking. Note that sections of the trail have loose gravel and can be sketchy in bad weather. Make sure you're wearing appropriate shoes and check conditions carefully. You can arrange a shuttle with [Trekkers] from Taupo that drops you at one end of the trail and picks you up at the other.

On the second day, stop at the famous Huka Falls, known for its bright blue color and the fact that six Olympic swimming pools worth of water flow through it every second! It only takes 20 minutes to visit, but is 100% worth the detour. In the evening, the sunset sail on Lake Taupo is a great way to wind down. You sail past enormous Māori rock carvings on the cliff face of the lake that are only visible from the water. And if you need a dinner, Taupo is home to the only McDonald's in the world located in an airplane!
[picture]
12–14
Wellington — The Coolest Little Capital
The next stop is New Zealand's capital, Wellington which is worth spending a couple days in. The capital has a genuine hipster edge, great food, cable cars, botantical gardens and is very walkable. Te Papa Tongarewa, the national museum, is the best museum in the country with an extremely well currated set of exhibits on the country's Māori heritage, extinct species and New Zealand's involvement in World War 1. Walk around the harbor area and spend an evening on Cuba Street, which has a great concentration of bars, independent coffee shops and street art. There's also a short hike from downtown to a sunset park viewpoint where you can look out over the whole city and the surrounding bay.
[picture]
15
Interislander Ferry → South Island
The best way to cross between the North and South Island is the Interislander ferry from Wellington to Picton. This three-hour crossing through the Marlborough Sounds is a scenic experience. Admire the beautiful blue-green water and coastline (a taste of what's to come in the South Island. There's not much to do in Picton, so keep moving once you arrive.
[picture]
16
Abel Tasman National Park
Abel Tasman is a must while in New Zealand with a coastline of white-gold beaches and bright turquoise water with a backdrop of lush green forests. It is home to the Abel Tasman Coastal Track is one of New Zealand's Great Walks and takes 3–4 days. If you're short on time, you can do an amazing one-day version. There are various water taxi services that will drop hikers at many points along the trail. I was dropped off in the gorgeous Anchorage Bay and then hiked back along the coast. Along the way, I stopped at a natural swimming hole called Cleopatra's Pools with a natural rock slide. The trail itself is full of wildlife including seals and wild goats. 

I chose to base myself in the small town of Kaiteriteri, sitting right on the edge of the national park, but Marahau is also a popular option as it is the official start of the Abel Tasman Coastal Track. There's also great kayaking around the famous Split Apple Rock, and the main beach at Kaiteriteri is excellent for a swim.
[picture]
17
West Coast
The West Coast between Abel Tasman and Franz Josef is a more remote portion of the country and I didn't find much to do here. The one exception is the Pancake Rocks and Blowhole at Paparoa National Park: extraordinary layered limestone formations right on the coast where the sea surges up through natural blowholes. It's the clear highlight of the West Coast and worth stopping for a couple hours when you're driving by.
[picture]
18–19
Franz Josef Glacier
Franz Josef is a small town that sits at the base of a massive glacier on the Southern Alps which decends into the rainforest below. The town has a lovely ski resort feel (think Aspen, but smaller and less posh). The standout activity is the Franz Josef Glacier Heli-Hike: a helicopter flight up to the upper glacier followed by a guided walk across the ice through crevasses and ice formations with the mountains all around. It's an amazing experience, but it is highly weather dependent so keep your schedule flexible in order to make sure you're able to do it. 

The town is also home to a number of amazing hikes all accessible from town. Two popular hikes are: The [fox glacier hike] which is [ ] long and leads you right up to the base of the Franz Josef Glacier. The [ ] mile (or [ ]km) rainforest hike with waterfalls everywhere and a beautiful river running through dramatic red rocks.

20–21
Queenstown — The Adventure Capital of the World
Queenstown is where most people spend the most time in New Zealand and there is a good reason why. The town lies on the shore of Lake Wakatipu dotted with ski-resort style wooden buildings. Queenstown is surrounded by amazing scenery, every adventure sport you can think of, great restaurants and a buzzing atmosphere even off-season. You could easily spend 5–7 days here.

Milford Sound — often called the eighth natural wonder of the world. I would recommend doing a full-day tour from Queenstown: you get picked up early and drive through Fjordland National Park, with several stops to admire the scenery like a black mirror-like reflective lakes along the way. When you arrive at Milford Sound, the best way to experience it is on a boat cruise. The fjord itself has sheer cliff faces rising from dark water with massive 10-story waterfalls on all sides. [Milford Sound is home to the highest concentration of waterfalls in the world.]

Routeburn Track — is one of New Zealand's Great Walks which stretches for [ ] miles. It takes [ ] days to do the full trek. Located only 1.5 hours away from Queenstown, the start of the trek is easily accessible and follows a stream of bright turquoise water straight from the glaciers leading to waterfalls, black mountain lakes, stone mountain huts and eventually snowcapped peaks. The hike is also possible as a day hike ([from the parking lot ot Harris Saddle]), which is a great out-and-back.

For adventure sports, Queenstown has everything: skydiving (you can see both ends of New Zealand from the air), bungee jumping at the highest commercial point in the Southern Hemisphere, the gondola up to Skyline for views over the lake and town, and a luge back down the hill from the gondola.

If you have extra time or want to extend the route, a few places worth knowing about. I've included links to other guides so you can do more research and craft an itinerary that works for you:

  • Roy's Peak —  a sunrise hike with extraordinary orange and purple light over the lake from the summit
  • Kaikōura — the whale watching capital of New Zealand on the east coast. Sperm whales are spotted year-round and the town has one of the highest concentrations of dolphins and whales in the world
  • Bay of Islands — a subtropical bay three hours north of Auckland with 144 islands and white sand beaches
  • Aoraki / Mount Cook — New Zealand's highest peak at 3724m. The Hooker Valley Track is one of the best accessible alpine hikes in the country

When to Visit

New Zealand is in the Southern Hemisphere, so its seasons are the reverse of North America and Europe. The best time to visit is during the New Zealand summer (December to February), when temperatures are warmest, the days are longest and most activities and trails are fully open. That said, this is also peak tourist season. Accommodation books up quickly for December and January, particularly in Queenstown and around the Milford Sound area.

The shoulder seasons of October to November and March to April are excellent alternatives. Expect fewer crowds, lower prices and still good weather. Winter (June to August) brings snow to the mountains and is prime ski season in Queenstown and Wanaka, making it a great time to visit if winter sports are your thing. However, many of the hiking trails will be closed during the winter.

Getting There

[picture]

New Zealand is a long, long way from almost anywhere so it is worth planning to stay for at least a few weeks. From the U.S., the flight is about 12–20 hours depending on if you're flying direct and coming from the West or East Coast. From Europe, expect a flight time of 24–28 hours including one or two layovers. Even from its nearest neighbor, Australia, expect a 3-hour flight from Sydney or Melbourne. Air New Zealand, Qantas and Jetstar are the main carriers to look out for when flying into New Zealand.

Auckland International Airport (AKL) is the main international gateway, with connections to most major hubs worldwide. Queenstown Airport (ZQN) is also a good entry point if you plan to start your trip on the South Island. One useful option if you're doing both the North and South Island is to fly into Auckland and fly out of Queenstown (or vice versa). This avoids backtracking and works out well with the 21-day itinerary above.

Getting Around

[picture]

Getting around New Zealand is one of the most enjoyable parts of the trip. The roads are excellent, the scenery is extraordinary and the distances are manageable. Here are your main options:

  • Rental Car — this is the most flexible option. The roads are well-maintained and there is very little traffic relative to most countries (New Zealand is mostly populated by sheep!). The only thing to note is Kiwis drive on the left
  • Kiwi Experience — this is a bright green hop-on, hop-off bus that runs a few different routes connecting all the major stops on both islands. I did this and highly recommend it, particularly for solo travelers. New Zealand hostels tend not to be very social on their own, so the Kiwi Experience is where you'll actually meet people. They help coordinate lodging and excursions while you still maintain the flexibility of choosing how long you want to stay in each destination
  • Campervan / RV — van life is enormously popular in New Zealand. A great option if you want total flexibility and don't mind the van life experience
To get between the North and South island, the best ways are:
  • Interislander Ferry — the scenic three-hour ferry between Wellington and Picton passes through the Marlborough Sounds, connecting the North and South Island. You can either book individual passenger or vehicle tickets. Book your tickets through Interislander or Bluebridge
  • Domestic Flights — Air New Zealand runs frequent services between Auckland, Wellington, Christchurch and Queenstown. A good option if you're short on time and can be cheaper than the ferry when booked in advance

Budget Breakdown

New Zealand is not a cheap destination by any measure. Expect prices to be similar to traveling in the U.S. or Western Europe. That said, it is possible to travel on a budget if you stay in hostel dorms, buy groceries, rent a campervan or prioritize free natural attractions (of which there are many).

Safety

New Zealand consistently ranks as one of the top three safest countries in the world, making it one of the most welcoming destinations for travelers. Violent crime against tourists is extremely rare and the country is always in the top ten on the Global Peace Index. As a visitor, you are likely safer in New Zealand than you are at home so there is nothing to worry about! 

Unlike Australia where everything is trying to kill you, New Zealand famously has no snakes, bears, wolves or other predators. If you're planning multi-day hikes in the wilderness: carry appropriate gear, check weather forecasts and respect trail closures. The Department of Conservation (DOC) website is the essential planning resource for any outdoor activity in New Zealand. 

Visa Requirements

Citizens of many countries, including the United States, United Kingdom, European Union, Canada and Australia, do not require a visa for stays up to 90 days in New Zealand. However, most visitors do require a New Zealand Electronic Travel Authority (NZeTA), which must be obtained before departure. The NZeTA costs $17 NZD ($10 USD) via the app or $23 NZD ($14 USD) via the website, and is valid for multiple trips over two years. Visitors must also pay the International Visitor Conservation and Tourism Levy (IVL) of $35 NZD when applying for the NZeTA. 

Citizens of other countries, including China, India and many others, require a standard visitor visa. Processing times and requirements vary, so check the Immigration New Zealand website well in advance of your trip.

When you enter the country, be prepared for extremely strict biosecurity entry inspections to protect its ecosystem. Make sure to clean all your outdoor equipment and declare any food items. The guards at the Auckland Airport even inspected the bottom of my shoes! 

Currency

New Zealand uses the New Zealand Dollar (NZD), often abbreviated as NZ$. At the time of writing, NZ$1 is worth approximately USD $0.60 or GBP £0.47, though exchange rates fluctuate.

Cards are accepted virtually everywhere in New Zealand. It is one of the most cashless societies in the world and you will rarely need physical notes except at some remote farm shops or market stalls. Most ATMs are widely available in all towns.

Language

If you're this far into the guide... you probably already know that English is spoken in New Zealand. However, New Zealand actually has two official languages: English and Māori (te reo Māori). 

New Zealand has done a great job of preserving and respecting the Māori language and culture. You'll notice Māori names and signage everywhere throughout the country. Many of the country's most iconic place names are Māori: Aotearoa (New Zealand), Aoraki (Mount Cook), Rotorua, Whanganui, Taranaki.
