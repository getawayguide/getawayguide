Top 10 Things to Do in New Zealand

Volcanic crossings, glacier heli-hikes, Hobbiton and the world's most dramatic fjords — all in one extraordinary country

May 8, 2026

New Zealand is one of those places that refuses to let you make a bad decision. Point yourself in almost any direction and something extraordinary awaits — a volcano, a glacier, a fjord, a set from a major film franchise, or a grade 5 rapid. The hard part is knowing where to start.

We spent 21 days driving the length of both islands, and the list below reflects the ten experiences that stood out above everything else. Some are famous. Some are surprisingly overlooked. All of them are worth going out of your way for. Here are the ten experiences you won't want to miss!

1. Walk the Tongariro Alpine Crossing

[photo]

The Tongariro Alpine Crossing is consistently ranked as one of the best day hikes on the planet — and having done it, I have no argument with that. The 20.2km point-to-point track takes you through five completely distinct terrains in a single day, each one more otherworldly than the last: the flat tussock of Mangatepopo Valley, the lunar black gravel of South Crater, the vivid rust-orange approach to Red Crater at 1,886 m, and then the descent past the famous Emerald Lakes.

Those lakes — Ngā Rotopounamu — are the payoff. Three vivid turquoise-green volcanic craters whose colour comes from silica and minerals leached from the surrounding rock. Just beyond them sits the Blue Lake (Te Wai Whakaata o Te Rangihīroa), which is sacred to local Māori — do not enter it. We completed the crossing in around six hours at a comfortable pace. The track is one-way, so you'll need a shuttle from National Park Village — most operators charge NZD $35–40 and coordinate timing around the crossing's rhythm. Weather is the one thing that can ruin it — wind on the ridge can exceed 100 km/h — so check the dedicated DOC forecast the night before and have a backup plan. Read my full guide to the Tongariro Alpine Crossing here.

Tip: Start as early as possible — ideally before 7am. The crossing gets crowded through the middle of the day and early starters get the summit views to themselves.

2. Do the Glacier Heli-Hike in Franz Josef

[photo]
[photo]

The Franz Josef Glacier Heli-Hike is expensive — NZD $899 per person — it is weather-dependent, and it is completely worth every dollar. The helicopter flight alone is extraordinary: a low pass through a narrow mountain valley flanked by waterfalls, before landing directly on the glacier surface in a world of blue ice and silence.

Ground-level access to the glacier face was permanently closed in 2012 after rockfalls made it unsafe — the helicopter is now the only way to reach the ice. Once your guide straps crampons to your boots, you spend up to 2.5 hours on the glacier: squeezing through crevasses, between towering seracs, and into blue ice tunnels that glow an electric azure where the compressed ice has had all its air squeezed out. The glacier moves around 2 metres a day, so the terrain changes constantly — no two hikes follow the same route. All equipment is included in the price. More details in my full Franz Josef guide.

Tip: Book the heli-hike as early in your trip as possible, then rebook if weather cancels. The West Coast has some of the most unpredictable weather in New Zealand — build at least one buffer day into your itinerary.

3. Visit the Hobbiton Movie Set

[photo]

Even if you have never seen a single frame of Lord of the Rings or The Hobbit, a visit to the Hobbiton Movie Set is a genuinely wonderful experience. The detail is extraordinary: 44 hobbit holes built into the rolling green hillside of a working Waikato farm, each one a different size and decorated differently, with smoke rising from chimneys, laundry on the lines, and tiny vegetables growing in the gardens. Peter Jackson chose this specific farm because it looked like the Shire from the air.

The guided tour (about 90 minutes) ends with a complimentary drink at the Green Dragon Inn — a fully functioning pub where the music was composed exclusively for The Shire and is not available elsewhere. Adults NZD $130, youth (11–17) NZD $65, children under 10 free. Tours must be booked in advance; walk-ups are not accepted. Located about 45 minutes from Rotorua near Matamata. Read more in my Rotorua guide.

4. Tackle the Grade 5 Kaituna River in Rotorua

[photo]
[photo]

The Kaituna River just outside Rotorua runs a grade 5 whitewater course that drops over Tutea Falls — a 7-metre drop that is the highest commercially rafted waterfall in the world. The run wastes no time: the waterfall comes early, the raft drops between two giant boulders and goes vertical, and everyone ends up in the river. That is more or less guaranteed.

Our raft was punctured somewhere in the middle stretch, which the guides handled with practiced calm while we clung to rocks and waited. It made the whole thing feel genuinely wild rather than theme-park controlled. Kaituna Cascades, the original operator since 1991, charges around NZD $135 per person with shuttle included from Rotorua. Tours run approximately 2 hours on the water. More in my full Rotorua guide.

Tip: Wear clothes you don't mind destroying. Bring a waterproof bag for your phone. Do not bring your phone in your pocket.

5. Cruise Milford Sound

[photo]

Milford Sound is technically a fiord — glacier-carved, not river-carved — making the famous name a geographic misnomer. It stretches 16 km to the Tasman Sea, plunges to 512 metres deep, and has Mitre Peak rising 1,692 metres straight from the waterline in a single spike. Rudyard Kipling called it the eighth wonder of the world, and the label has stuck for good reason.

The drive from Queenstown takes around 4 hours and is itself worth the journey: Mirror Lakes that perfectly reflect the mountains above, The Chasm where the Cleddau River has carved extraordinary rock formations, and the Homer Tunnel — 1.2 km single-lane, blasted through the Darran Mountains starting in 1935 and only opened to traffic in 1954. On the water, cruise boats pull beneath Stirling Falls (146 m) close enough to drench you. Rain multiplies the waterfalls dramatically — don't let a grey forecast put you off going. RealNZ cruises from around NZD $145. More in my Queenstown guide.

Tip: Leave Queenstown by 7am to reach the 10:30am cruise. Rain makes Milford Sound better, not worse — hundreds of temporary waterfalls appear on wet days that don't exist in dry conditions.

6. Walk the Routeburn Track

[photo]
[photo]

The Routeburn Track is one of New Zealand's nine Great Walks — a 32km point-to-point trail crossing the boundary between Fiordland and Mount Aspiring National Parks. Done as a day hike from Queenstown, it takes roughly 7 hours at a steady pace, climbing from the beech forest through Routeburn Falls to Harris Saddle at 1,255 m.

The moment that stays with you is Lake Harris, a tannin-dark alpine tarn sitting just below the saddle — completely still, ringed by cirque walls, the water so dark it mirrors the sky at a colour unlike anything you'd expect at altitude. Tracknet Transport runs shuttles from Queenstown departing 8am (NZD ~$55 return). For the overnight version, Routeburn Falls Hut and Lake Mackenzie Hut must be booked through DOC's Great Walk system — they open in June for the following season and sell out fast. More in my Queenstown guide.

7. Kayak the Abel Tasman Coastal Track

[photo]

Abel Tasman is New Zealand's smallest national park (22,530 hectares) but arguably its most photogenic coastline: golden-sand beaches, warm turquoise water, granite headlands, and native bush that drops straight to the shore. The park sits at the top of the South Island near Nelson, with an unusually mild climate year-round. The best way to experience it is by sea kayak.

Multi-day kayaking trips let you paddle between beaches like Anchorage, Bark Bay, and Awaroa, camping on the shore and covering sections of coast completely inaccessible by land. Day trips from Marahau start around NZD $165 per person. Water taxis (from NZD $59 one-way) let you mix walking and paddling. The 60km Abel Tasman Coast Track is also a Great Walk that can be done in 3–5 days. Wildlife includes NZ fur seals, little blue penguins, and bottlenose dolphins. Note: the Awaroa tidal crossing must be timed within 1.5 hours before to 2 hours after low tide — missing the window means a 12-hour wait.

Tip: The park is most crowded in January and February. Visit in November or March for quieter beaches and the same conditions.

8. Visit Cathedral Cove on the Coromandel

[photo]
[photo]

The Coromandel Peninsula — about 2.5 hours from Auckland — is one of the most underrated sections of the North Island trip. Cathedral Cove (Mautohe) is the standout attraction: a massive natural limestone arch connecting two white-sand beaches, with a cave large enough to walk through and water clear enough to see the bottom from the surface. It appeared in The Chronicles of Narnia: Prince Caspian.

The walk from the Grange Road car park takes about 45 minutes each way through coastal bush. A water taxi from Hahei Beach (NZD $40 adult each way) drops you directly at the beach in 10 minutes — the better option as it lets you arrive early before the crowds. After Cathedral Cove, Hot Water Beach is 10 minutes away: geothermal fissures beneath the sand allow you to dig your own spa pool at low tide, with water reaching 64°C at the vent. Visit within 2 hours either side of low tide. Spades available nearby for NZD $10.

9. Spend a Morning at Te Papa Museum, Wellington

[photo]

Wellington is a genuinely excellent city — compact, walkable, full of independent cafes and bars, with a waterfront harbour and a creative scene that punches well above its population. The highlight for any visitor should be Te Papa Tongarewa, the national museum on the waterfront. International visitors pay NZD $35; it is enormous, and it covers Māori culture, natural history, Pacific identity and the country's colonial past with unusual depth and honesty.

The museum holds the only publicly displayed colossal squid in the world — caught in the Ross Sea in 2008, it is 4.2 metres long and weighs 495 kg. The Māori taonga collection — carved wharenui, weapons, cloaks, oral history — gives real cultural context to everything you've experienced travelling through the country. Plan for at least three hours; it's easy to spend a full day. Wellington is typically visited on the ferry crossing between islands — the Interislander or Bluebridge ferry takes around 3.5 hours across Cook Strait, with the final 45 minutes winding through the stunning Marlborough Sounds.

Tip: Weta Workshop in the Miramar suburb (20 mins from the city centre, 5 mins from the airport) offers 90-minute behind-the-scenes tours of the Lord of the Rings effects studio. Worth booking in advance.

10. Get Around on the Kiwi Bus

[photo]
[photo]

The hop-on hop-off backpacker bus — operated by Kiwi Experience and Stray Travel — connects all the main stops across both islands. You buy a pass valid for 12 months, get off wherever you like, stay as long as you want, and reboard the next bus heading the same direction. For solo travellers or small groups without a rental car, it is by far the best way to see the country. Kiwi Experience passes range from NZD $85 for short routes to NZD $1,200+ for unlimited all-country. Stray's comprehensive Cook Tour (23 days, top to bottom) runs NZD $4,345.

The main advantage over a campervan is social — the bus puts you next to other travellers doing the same loop, so you arrive at each destination already knowing people. Routes cover both islands and connect onto the Interislander ferry between Wellington and Picton. The campervan alternative gives more freedom but costs more solo (from NZD $75–220/day in peak season) and is better suited to couples or small groups who want to go entirely at their own pace. Book Kiwi Bus passes before you leave home in peak season (December–February) as popular legs fill up.
