Rotorua — Geothermal Wonders and Māori Culture

Hobbiton, grade 5 rapids, bubbling mud pools, and a town that smells distinctly of sulfur

May 2026

The Town

Rotorua is one of the most geothermally active places on Earth and the air confirms it as soon as you arrive — a permanent mild smell of sulphur drifts through town from the vents and pools scattered throughout the area. It is not unpleasant so much as otherworldly. The city is also one of the most significant centres of Māori culture in New Zealand, with several of the major attractions here operated by and deeply connected to the local Ngāti Whakaue iwi.

Most people spend 2–3 days here, and there is enough to fill them. Hobbiton, the rafting, Te Puia and the hot pools alone will more than use up your time.

Hobbiton

[photo]
[photo]

Hobbiton is the actual film set used for the Lord of the Rings and Hobbit trilogies, built on a working sheep farm about 65 km from Rotorua (around 45 minutes by car, or 2 hours from Auckland). There are 44 hobbit holes scattered across rolling green hills, each built with extraordinary detail — smoke rises from chimneys, tiny vegetables grow in the gardens, laundry hangs on washing lines, and the scale shifts between oversized and undersized depending on where the camera was meant to be pointing. Peter Jackson chose this specific farm in the Waikato because it looked like the Shire from the air.

The tour walks you through the entire set over about 90 minutes, ending at the Green Dragon Inn — a fully functional pub where a complimentary drink from the Hobbit Southfarthing range is included in the ticket. The music inside was composed exclusively for The Shire and is not available anywhere else. I went in with reasonably neutral expectations and left genuinely impressed. The craftsmanship is extraordinary, and being inside a place you've seen in films has a strange quality to it that is hard to describe until you experience it.

[photo]

Tours must be pre-booked — walk-ups are not accepted and the farm is private property. Prices: NZD $130 adult, $65 youth (11–17), free for children under 10 (still requires a ticket). Total tour duration including coach transfer from The Shire's Rest visitor centre is approximately 2.5 hours.

Tip: Book as far in advance as possible. Peak season tours sell out weeks ahead. The Evening Banquet tour is an elevated version with a three-course dinner in The Green Dragon — worth it for a special occasion.

White Water Rafting

[photo]

The Kaituna River just outside Rotorua runs through a gorge lined with tree ferns and native bush, over grade 5 rapids, and down Tutea Falls — a 7-metre drop that is the highest commercially rafted waterfall in the world. The river is an extraordinary shade of blue-green and the scenery alone would make it worthwhile even without the adrenaline. The raft drops between two giant boulders into a wide, deep pool, and everyone goes in.

Our raft got punctured partway through on a rock and partially deflated, which added an unplanned element of chaos to the rapids. We made it fine, but the guides are very good and the experience felt genuinely wild rather than theme-park controlled. This is one of the best things to do in Rotorua. Tours run approximately 2 hours on the water. Kaituna Cascades — the original operator since 1991 — charges around NZD $135 per person, with shuttles included from the Rotorua CBD.

Te Puia

[photo]
[photo]

Te Puia is operated by Ngāti Whakaue and is the main cultural and geothermal attraction in Rotorua. Its centrepiece is Pōhutu Geyser — the largest active geyser in the Southern Hemisphere — which erupts up to 20 times a day, shooting boiling water up to 30 metres into the air. Each eruption lasts 10–20 minutes, which means most visitors are very likely to see it in action. Around it sit dozens of bubbling mud pools in various sizes and colours, hissing steam vents, and silica terraces built up over centuries by mineral-rich water.

The park also houses the New Zealand Māori Arts and Crafts Institute, where you can watch students working on traditional carving — wood, stone and bone — and flax weaving. The craft school has been operating since 1963 and seeing the work in progress gives the cultural element genuine weight. Entry is NZD $105 adult, $52.50 child; guided tours run hourly from 9am to 4pm.

Kiwi Hatchery

Te Puia also contains a kiwi conservation centre where you can see the birds up close in a darkened nocturnal house. Kiwi are extraordinary animals — five species, all endangered — and the eggs are so enormous relative to the female's body size that a single egg fills around 20% of her body cavity. The male incubates it for up to 80 days. The hatchery actively works to increase survival rates through managed breeding and predator control.

Seeing a kiwi in real life is genuinely moving. They are much larger than most people expect, surprisingly fast, and make an odd snuffling sound as they root around for insects. The nocturnal house is dimly lit to keep the birds calm, so photography is limited, but your eyes adjust quickly.

Kuirau Hot Pools

[photo]

Kuirau Park is a free public geothermal reserve right on the edge of Rotorua's CBD — New Zealand's only free geothermal public park. There are three foot-soaking pools, bubbling mud pools, boiling waterfalls, hissing steam vents, and vibrantly coloured mineral pools. At sunset the light through the rising mist makes the whole place feel completely surreal. The sulphur smell is strongest here, but it becomes oddly pleasant after a few minutes. A good way to end an afternoon before dinner — and free, which is rare in a town that charges for most things.

Also worth considering: Wai-O-Tapu Thermal Wonderland, 30 km south of Rotorua, is arguably the most visually spectacular geothermal park in New Zealand. The Champagne Pool (65m across, 73°C, formed by a hydrothermal explosion ~800 years ago) and the Lady Knox Geyser — which erupts daily at exactly 10:15am, triggered by a surfactant dropped into the vent — are both extraordinary. Entry NZD $45 adult.
