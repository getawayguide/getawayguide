Top 10 Things to Do in El Salvador

Volcano hikes, surf breaks, hidden waterfalls, and the most extraordinary sunsets in Central America

Apr 16, 2026

El Salvador packs a remarkable variety of experiences into an incredibly small country. You can drive from end to end in just five hours — yet within that distance you'll find active volcanoes, hidden hot spring waterfalls, colonial highland towns, black-sand surf breaks, and some of the most extraordinary sunsets in Central America. Here are the ten experiences I'd recommend to anyone visiting.

1. Take on the 7 Waterfalls Hike

[Photo]

This is, without question, my favourite experience in all of El Salvador — and one of the best adventure days I've had anywhere in Central America. The 7 Waterfalls Hike (Los Chorros de La Calera) takes place near Juayúa on the Ruta de las Flores and leads you through dense jungle and volcanic terrain to a series of seven stunning cascades, each one different in height, force, and character.

The hike itself takes around 3 to 4 hours return and involves some river crossing and scrambling, which is part of the fun. Several of the waterfalls have natural pools deep enough to swim in — locals leap from the rocks above and you're welcome to join. A local guide costs around $10–15 per person and is well worth it for navigation and safety. Most hostels in Juayúa or Ataco can arrange a guide for you. Read my full guide to the 7 Waterfalls Hike here.

Tip: Go early — trails are quieter and the light through the jungle canopy is at its best in the morning. Wear shoes you don't mind getting wet.

2. Surf in El Tunco

[Photo]

El Tunco is El Salvador's backpacker surf hub — a small, lively beach town built around a consistent right-hand beach break on a dramatic stretch of black volcanic sand. The waves here are some of the most reliable in Central America, making it equally appealing for complete beginners taking their first lesson and experienced surfers looking for a fast, punchy wave.

The town itself is small enough to walk everywhere in ten minutes, with a string of surf schools, board rentals, beach bars, and hostels strung along the main drag. Lessons run around $30–40 for two hours including a board and instructor. If you already surf, board rentals start at around $10 per day. Read my full guide to El Tunco here.

Tip: The dry season (November to April) brings offshore winds and cleaner conditions. Wet season waves can be bigger and more powerful — better for experienced surfers.

3. Hike the Santa Ana Volcano

[Photo]

The Santa Ana Volcano — known locally as Ilamatepec — is the highest active volcano in El Salvador and one of the most rewarding hikes in Central America. The highlight is the crater lake: a vivid turquoise pool sitting in the caldera, its otherworldly color caused by high concentrations of sulfur and volcanic minerals. Steaming fumaroles ring the crater rim and the views across western El Salvador stretch all the way to Guatemala on a clear day.

The hike is around 4.4 miles (7.1 km) return and takes approximately 4 hours. El Salvador requires hikers to be accompanied by a government-approved guide ($4 per person), and guides only depart at set times — plan ahead. Most hostels in Santa Ana can arrange a tour that also includes a stop at Lago de Coatepeque, the brilliant blue crater lake 45 minutes away. Read my full guide to Santa Ana here.

Tip: Start early — guides typically depart around 8am. Bring layers; the summit can be surprisingly cold and windy.

4. Learn About El Salvador's Recent History with Hector's Walking Tour

[Photo]

Of all the things I did in El Salvador, Hector's Walking Tour in Santa Ana was one of the most unexpectedly moving. Hector is a local guide who has lived through every chapter of El Salvador's recent transformation — from the height of gang violence to the extraordinary change that followed President Bukele's crackdown starting in 2022. He tells it with remarkable candour, balance, and warmth.

The tour winds through Santa Ana's historic center — the cathedral, the national theater, the market, the old social clubs — while Hector layers in the social and political history behind each building. You leave with a much richer understanding of why this country is the way it is, and why so many Salvadorans speak about the changes with such complicated, genuine emotion. The tour runs for around 2–3 hours and costs a very reasonable tip-based fee. More details in my Santa Ana guide.

Tip: Book in advance through your hostel or via WhatsApp — Hector's tours are popular and spots fill up, especially in peak season.

5. Visit the Natural Phenomenon El Salto de Malacatiupán

[Photo]

El Salto de Malacatiupán is one of the most unusual natural attractions in El Salvador — a series of hot spring waterfalls that cascade directly into a river, where the hot volcanic water meets the cool mountain current. The contrast creates a range of natural pools at different temperatures, each one fed by geothermal springs rising through the volcanic rock. It feels less like a tourist attraction and more like a genuine natural wonder that most visitors to the country completely miss.

Located about an hour outside of Santa Ana, the site is best reached by car, Uber, or taxi. Entry is very affordable at around $3–5 per person. Bring a swimsuit and plan to spend at least two hours here — the combination of swimming, warm water, and jungle surroundings makes it easy to lose track of time. More details in my Santa Ana guide.

Tip: Weekdays are much quieter. On weekends, Salvadoran families come in large groups and the pools can get crowded.

6. Get Off the Grid in Playa Shalpa

[Photo]

After the energy of El Tunco, Playa Shalpa feels like another world — and that's exactly the point. Located less than an hour up the coast, this off-the-beaten-path stretch of Pacific coastline has none of El Tunco's crowds or noise, just a long black-sand beach, calm energy, and a handful of small guesthouses and eco-retreats tucked into the palms. It's the kind of place where the most pressing decision of the day is whether to sit by the pool or walk down to the water.

Playa Shalpa is perfect for the final days of a trip — somewhere to decompress before flying home, or to simply slow down after a packed itinerary. Given how small El Salvador is, it's easy to combine with El Tunco or to use as a base for a day trip back along the coast. There are no ATMs and limited phone signal, so bring cash and embrace the disconnection.

Tip: Accommodation options are limited, so book ahead. A few small boutique guesthouses fill quickly during dry season weekends.

7. Take a Pupusa Making Class

[Photo]

The pupusa is El Salvador's national dish and cultural symbol — a thick handmade corn tortilla stuffed with cheese, beans, chicharrón (pork), or loroco (a local flower bud), cooked on a flat griddle and served with curtido (fermented cabbage slaw) and tomato salsa. Once you eat your first one, you will understand why Salvadorans are so proud of them. At $0.50–$1.00 each from a street vendor, they are also one of the great bargains of any trip.

Several hostels and local operators in Santa Ana and Juayúa offer hands-on pupusa making classes where a local woman teaches you to mix the masa, shape the dough, stuff it, and cook it on the comal. The class takes about 90 minutes, ends with eating everything you've made, and costs around $15–25 per person. It is one of the most genuinely enjoyable and culturally immersive things you can do in the country. More details in my Santa Ana guide.

Tip: Book through your hostel reception — they typically work with the same local instructors and can arrange small group sessions.

8. Ride a Motorcycle Between the Towns of Ruta de las Flores

[Photo]

The Ruta de las Flores connects five small colonial highland towns — Nahuizalco, Salcoatitán, Juayúa, Apaneca and Concepción de Ataco — along a winding mountain road through coffee farms, pine forests and wildflower-covered hillsides. The towns are only 10–15 minutes apart, and the road between them is one of the most scenic in the country. Riding it by motorcycle is simply the best way to do it.

Several operators in Juayúa and Ataco rent motorbikes for the day for around $30–50, or you can join an organised motorcycle group tour. No prior experience is required for the smaller 125cc bikes on this flat-ish route. If you're not confident on a motorbike, renting a bicycle or taking a tuk-tuk between towns is a solid alternative. Read my full guide to Ruta de las Flores here.

Tip: Start in Nahuizalco in the south and work north to Ataco — you finish in the most scenic and lively town, which makes for a perfect ending to the day.

9. Watch a Miraculous Sunset in El Tunco

[Photo]

The sunsets in El Tunco are extraordinary — and that is not an overstatement. The combination of the black volcanic sand, the open Pacific horizon, and the particular quality of light at this latitude produces skies that seem almost artificially vivid: deep oranges bleeding into reds and purples, with the silhouette of the famous El Tunco rock formation in the foreground. Every evening feels like a performance.

The beach fills up naturally around 5pm as surfers paddle in and travellers wander down from their guesthouses. The best viewing spots are from the beach itself or from one of the raised terrace bars that overlook the shore — grab a cold Pilsener or a fresh coconut and settle in. No phone filter required. More in my El Tunco guide.

Tip: The rock formation at the southern end of the beach frames the sunset particularly well — position yourself there about 20 minutes before the sun drops.

10. Visit the Quirky Cafe Albania

[Photo]

Perched in the highlands above Apaneca on the Ruta de las Flores, Cafe Albania is one of El Salvador's more unusual attractions — a working coffee farm that has quietly become an adventure playground. The cafe itself is beautiful: open-air terraces with sweeping mountain views, excellent local coffee grown on the surrounding hillside, and a genuine commitment to sustainable farming. But what sets it apart is the collection of unexpected activities on-site.

Visitors can ride a zip-line across the coffee fields, career down a giant slide through the trees, cycle a tandem bike along the ridgeline, or simply sit with a coffee and watch the clouds move through the valley below. Entry is free or very low-cost, with individual activities priced separately. It has the energy of something dreamed up by someone who genuinely loves where they live — which, it turns out, is exactly what it is. More in my Ruta de las Flores guide.

Tip: Go on a weekday if possible — weekends bring large local crowds. Allow at least 2–3 hours to properly explore the farm and activities.
